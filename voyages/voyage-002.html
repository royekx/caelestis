Full Account The whole story, as written

The second lesson of Caelestis began at a desk.

Boatswain Tarto finished the line she was writing before she looked up. A new cadet stood waiting — Bartholomew, a wilder, recently arrived, orientation already behind him. She told him what he was without making it a question, and then told him it changed nothing once he was aboard a crew. She named the Weeping Goddess and the lunch hour, recommended the dragon scale pizza — charred pepperoni laid out like scales over a body of dough shaped to suggest something more dangerous than it was — and pointed him toward both. His crew would already be gathering. Then she returned to her ledger, and the conversation was over.

The Weeping Goddess

The Weeping Goddess was full. Tabaxi, loxodons, hadozee, autognomes, thri-kreen, faces drawn from every corner of wildspace, all moving and eating and talking past one another beneath blue-glowing orbs while Petty Officer Winston Ryeback held the kitchen at a bellow that carried over the noise. The only open seat Bartholomew could find was at a table with one other cadet — a slight boy who met his eye and gestured him in.

His name was Miken. He was nervous in a way that did not quite hide itself, and he spoke about the simulation as though they had both been through it, correcting himself, embarrassed, when Bartholomew let him understand he had not. The conversation drifted to safer ground from there: cadet life, the training rotation, what was supposedly coming next. There was an empty chair at the table neither of them named, but Miken's eye returned to it more than once.

The rest of the cadets came in piecemeal. Gregory entered first, with the others orbiting his pace as they had begun to do without naming it. Boogie and Sol scanned the room and found the only table with open seats — the one with Bartholomew and Miken already at it. They sat. The introductions were light, friendly enough, but Bartholomew did not quite step toward them and they did not quite step toward him. None of them had been told by name what the sixth looked like. He had been told they would be five, and there they were, but the moment of recognition stayed soft and unspoken.

At the back of the refectory, a board crowded with handwritten notices ran along one wall — well-wishes pinned for crews already out among the stars, odd jobs for available hands, requests from the docks, the occasional missing-item notice. Tumak studied it with the patience of someone looking for something specific. Casey drifted to it as well. Neither found quite what they were looking for. Tumak found nothing familiar from home. Casey, his hand brushing past offers for cargo work and freight, pulled one for the Sea Dock without explaining why he wanted it.

Ryeback came to the table himself. The first round of bowls was already in his hands before the cadets had settled, and he distributed them with affectionate brusqueness — eat, all of you — pausing long enough to take new orders from those who wanted something else. Bartholomew asked for the dragon scale pizza. Sol asked for soup and was given a steaming bowl Ryeback called knock-your-mama's-socks-off soup, a name he gave without irony. Boogie asked only for fruit. What Ryeback set in front of him was something none of them had seen before: a silvery starfruit the color of moonlight on metal, like a kiwi rendered in pale silver.

When Ryeback returned, Tumak asked for two meatloafs. Gregory, who had not yet ordered, asked what else there might be, and was offered a set of spare utensils — not a slight, Gregory could see when he looked carefully, but a galley cook trying to be kind to an autognome and not knowing what else to offer. He accepted the gesture.

It was Casey who pulled Ryeback into something different. The cook mentioned, almost in passing, that someone named Barret had been at the counter the night before running his mouth about a hadozee who could read birthdays. Casey, suddenly interested, asked Ryeback for his — and then, when the answer did not come immediately, asked him something more personal: what he did after work, what he kept for himself. With patience and unexpected warmth, Casey drew it out of him. Ryeback liked hot baths. Ryeback liked to knit. Casey looked at the bowls of soup and stew and the man who had brought them, and said something quiet about how both made things whole, one piece at a time. Ryeback paused. Then he gave Casey an extra portion and moved on.

Miken stayed long enough to feel like part of the table. Boogie leaned over to Sol at one point and said, low enough that the rest could half-hear, that Miken might be a better fit for their crew than the one they had been given. They laughed about it. Then, gently, Boogie let Miken know they had things to discuss as a crew, and Miken — graciously, easily — picked up his bowl and went. He understood. He left.

When the table was theirs again, Boogie did the thing none of them had quite been ready for. He reached into his own chest and brought out the half-missing model ship he had pulled from himself the night before, set it down between them, and let them look at it. Tumak, considering it, said quietly that things had been going missing all over the academy lately, and somebody at this table might want to think about what that meant. He did not say more. The half-ship sat on the table for a long moment. Nobody picked up quite what Boogie had set down. Casey laid the cargo notice from the Sea Dock beside it. Nobody picked that up either.

The conversation turned eventually to the roles they would take on a real ship, and the cadets began to sort themselves — Tumak for the captain's chair, Gregory for the navigator's, Boogie speaking for the medical role, Casey for the guns. The spelljammer's seat stayed open for a while. Boogie quietly insisted that Gregory should take it, despite Gregory having shown no particular pull toward the helm. He did not explain why. The position went unsettled.

Tumak got up to use the bathroom. Casey followed him without explanation, slipping through the refectory unseen. He waited inside the door long enough to hear movement on the other side of the stalls — rummaging, the careful sounds of someone collecting themselves — and when Tumak came out, his jacket was a little askew before he straightened it. Casey said nothing. Tumak said nothing. They returned to the table separately. When Ryeback came by with the second of Tumak's two meatloafs, no one but Casey thought to notice.

The Missing Ship

Boogie wanted to try the helm again. The conversation that followed pulled at several of them — Tumak stayed behind at the Weeping Goddess — but Boogie, Gregory, and Bartholomew sought out Tarto and asked if they could use the Simulation Deck. She told them they did not need a simulation chamber simply to practice. She took them to the Spelljammer Nexus instead and sent for Saerth Abyzine.

Saerth arrived as the cadets had seen him before — silver-eyed, calm, possessed of an old eloquence that made every word feel deliberate. He agreed to put them through another helm exercise. Then, almost without warning, that composure cracked. Someone had taken his miniature spelljamming ship — his, by name, his particular vessel — and the voice that came out of him was sharp enough that for a beat the room held still. He caught himself almost immediately. The composure returned. He apologized, briefly, and moved on as though it had not happened.

Boogie heard the rest of the exercise as if from underwater. Saerth had not been speaking to him at all. But the ship Saerth had lost — a teacher's careful miniature, missing in some manner not yet explained — was the same model that had come out of Boogie's own chest the night before. He did not say so. He did the exercise. Bartholomew, untroubled by any such weight, made the helm respond as if he had been waiting his whole life for the asking. When the time came to settle who would sit in the spelljammer's chair, the question answered itself.

The Sea Dock

While the helm exercise unfolded in the Nexus, Casey took the cargo notice he had pulled from the board and walked it down to the Sea Dock. He was looking, in some quiet part of himself, for Joffrey — the sailor he had spoken with the day before about a life among the stars — and he wanted to ask him something about hadozee birth signs, something about reading more than three stars at once, something about whatever might lie beyond the limit of his own gift. But he confused the docks. Joffrey was not at the Sea Dock. The dock Casey arrived at instead was run by a different man entirely.

Rindle Gearloft kept his attention fixed elsewhere until Casey had been standing long enough to demand it. When he finally looked up, he was already barking — orders, instructions, a flat sequence of where to put what and how. Casey worked the cargo for the better part of an hour without complaint. When he eventually mentioned that he had pulled the job from the refectory board, Rindle hesitated, apologized that he had not realized a cadet was filling it, and quietly paid him for the time.

Casey climbed up to the Sky Dock afterward — the Sky Dock, where Joffrey actually was, though Casey did not find him there. He stood at the rail instead, looking out into the astral dark. He thought of his brother. He sat with that for a long time, and at some point during it, he became aware that he was no longer alone. The same familiar presence that had spoken to him from inside Mirt's impossible wardrobe had settled beside him without sound or weight. He did not look. By the time he got up to return to his bunk, the presence had gone again.

Night in the Quarters

The cadets returned to their bunks one by one. Tumak fell asleep first, and he snored with an immediacy and volume that filled the entire chamber. After a while it became clear to anyone listening that the rhythm was strange — almost as if two breaths were fighting to share a single sleep, the inhales never quite landing on the same beat. Cadets nearby pulled pillows over their heads. Eventually Miken, two bunks over, sat up, murmured an incantation that did not quite belong to a cadet of his year, and threw a small bubble of silence over the offending stretch of the room. The rest of the night passed.

The Salvage Race

In the morning they were summoned to the Simulation Deck. The other crew was already there — Miken, Vena, Barret, and the rest of their squad — assigned to the same exercise from the opposite chamber. Saerth and Tarto ran the briefing together. A salvage objective: a logbook aboard a derelict vessel adrift in an asteroid field. Two crews, one race. The first ship back with the prize would carry the score. The cadets entered their pods. The simulation took hold. The deck of a hammerhead unfolded beneath their feet, the void waiting beyond the rail.

Bartholomew laid his hand on the helm and the ship answered. The hammerhead lifted away from the launch — fast, clean — and from there the rest of them found their stations. Tumak at the captain's chair, calling movement; Gregory at the navigator's chart, plotting the course; Boogie tending the bulwark; Casey at one of the ballistae; Sol moving through the deck with the ease of a man who had spent years fitting himself into other people's plans and had finally been given one to share. The rival ship lagged at takeoff. The lead was theirs to lose.

Gregory's chart resolved a clean line toward the asteroid field. The route ran them near an electrical storm — vast clouds of low light, silver eels twisting through them like muscle made of lightning — and when the storm came into view, the crew chose to push through it rather than around. The other ship was behind. The other ship would have to choose the long way. The hammerhead took the storm head-on. The eels lashed at the hull. The bulwark held, mostly, with Boogie steadying what the storm peeled away. The ship rolled through the worst of it and came out the other side with damage to spare but the lead intact. The asteroid field opened in front of them. The wreck waited at its heart.

They approached the derelict across a chain of asteroids, leaping between them in turn. Tumak crossed. Casey crossed. Gregory crossed. Sol crossed. Boogie — several rocks out from the ship — misjudged his footing. A drift of pale gossamer mist obscured the next rock just as he committed to the jump. The asteroid slid out of his line of sight. The plasmoid's trajectory began to drift.

On the deck of the hammerhead, Sol felt it before the others saw it. Some part of him already knew that misfortune was reaching for one of his crewmates. He did not hesitate. He gave it to himself instead. Strength left him in a measured drop — not enough to drop him with it, but enough to feel. And as it left, somewhere beneath the surface of his hearing, a woman laughed. Quiet, amused, far away. Sol had not heard her before. He did not know who she was. But the laugh repeated itself once, twice more in the moments that followed, and by the third time he understood. Beshaba. He would not forget the sound now that he had it.

Boogie, who should have missed the jump, did not. He landed clean on the asteroid he had been aiming for and could not entirely explain to himself why he had thought he would not. Gregory and Bartholomew, watching from the deck, both caught it: an arc of black lightning thin as a hair, flickering out of Sol's frame and snapping back into him as quickly as it had appeared. They saw him wince.

The crew split as they came aboard the wreck. Tumak, Casey, and Boogie made their way below deck. Sol, Gregory, and Bartholomew held the topside. The lower corridors were dark, lit by nothing, and the bones of the vessel creaked beneath their boots. The captain's quarters were where the cadets expected to find what they had come for. They searched the desk first and did not find it. The logbook lay instead in the far corner of the room, half-tucked behind the body of a long-decomposed creature in a curled, broken position — something that might once have been crew, or might once have been something else entirely. None of them stopped to investigate it. The simulation did not invite the question. Boogie collected the logbook, considered it for a moment, and then — without quite explaining the impulse to himself — slipped it inside his own chest.

The Ambush

Above deck, Gregory's eye on the dark caught the second ship before its silhouette had fully resolved. Not the cadet rivals they had been racing. A githyanki vessel, slipping through the asteroid field as if it had always been there, drawing in fast. Gregory shouted down the hatch — now, hurry — and the below-deck team moved.

Casey, thinking faster than any of them, asked Boogie to bring the logbook back out. Boogie did. Casey bound a duplicate of the pages into being, and Gregory, beside him, immediately followed with a casting of his own. Three identical logbooks lay between them across the deck. Bartholomew, working at the rail, called a fog cloud down over the hull like a blanket.

The githyanki ship came at them through the mist. Bartholomew threw the helm into motion. The hammerhead lurched, turned, began to pull away. The ballistae found their range. Tumak called commands; Gregory named targets and openings; Casey fired across the gap. Boogie, who should have stayed at the bulwark, did not. The plasmoid threw himself across the space between the two ships, landed clean on the enemy deck, and dropped through the hatch into the lower corridors of a ship he did not know.

It became clear, faster than Boogie wanted it to, that he had brought himself into more than he could finish alone. He turned around. On the hammerhead, Bartholomew watched it happen. The ship was already pulling toward safety, but Bartholomew slowed it — held the angle, eased the speed, gave Boogie a target to come back to. Tumak grabbed a coil of rope and fired it across the gap, hoping to land a line for the plasmoid to catch. The throw missed by half a body's length. Gregory swung the ballista around and put a clean shot through the enemy hull — covering fire, however unnecessary it would prove. None of it had been strictly required. All of it had been offered. Boogie threw himself back across the void and landed on the rear deck of the hammerhead. The fight resolved. The hammerhead pulled away.

By the time they came within sight of the academy's simulated dock, Miken's crew was visible off the port quarter — drifting, damaged, signaling for help. Tumak turned to Gregory and asked, straight-faced, whether the cadet manual contained any provision on the etiquette of stopping for stranded vessels. Gregory considered the question with the same seriousness it demanded. He thought it through. He concluded, after a moment, that no such provision appeared to exist.
