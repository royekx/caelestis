The Remaking

Vecna's plan, named. The antagonist's throughline — the mirror of the crew's Voyages. This page holds the durable structure: what the plan is, how it works, who carries it out, and where it stands. The living state — node-by-node progress and how the players have perturbed it — is tracked in the Campaign Tracker and logged below.

Two throughlines, one campaign Voyages is what the crew did, session by session. The Remaking is what the plan did, node by node. The crew's actions perturb the Remaking; the Remaking's moves pressure the crew. They are the campaign's call and response — read them side by side.
Who is actually doing this Vecna acts through no organisation of his own. The work of the Remaking is carried out by parties who want their own things: the Seekers, who were taught how to ask, and the Vth'orama, who are building an empire. Neither serves him knowingly. Neither could be told to stop by anyone he could name.

Inscription

Vecna's working is a cosmic inscription — but his role in it is more subtle than the metaphor suggests. He does not write Monoliths. The Continuum writes the Monoliths. What Vecna does is read the universe as a map, decide what shape he wants drawn across it, and arrange for the dots to land where his shape requires.

The picture Imagine you could stand outside the cosmos and see every Monolith in three dimensions at once. Vecna has chosen a shape — a spell-circle, a binding diagram, an arcane figure — that, if drawn at cosmic scale by the right Monoliths in the right positions, functions as a spell. Each Monolith is a point on that figure. Some already stand exactly where he needs them. Others don't, but the world that would raise one is approaching a Zenith of its own and needs only a nudge to cross at the right moment.

He didn't invent the system. He didn't manufacture the cosmology. He's the first being known to step back far enough to see the system as a pattern, and the first to decide what figure he could draw using only the dots the universe will provide if asked nicely.

Vecna's position in the Continuum DM understanding

Working background for DM use. Player-discoverable late, probably Arc III, never assumed.

Vecna's ascension to godhood was a Zenith. He is a later god: he ascended through Transformation, and a Monolith manifested in response. At the time, he didn't understand what had happened. He was busy being newly divine.

But godhood gave him room to look back. The thing that became Vecna noticed, over time, that there is machinery beneath gods — that the appearance of that Monolith at his ascension was not a coincidence and not a gift, but a response, by something larger than him. Most gods never see this. They live inside the Continuum without recognizing it; they know their portfolios, their followers, their rivals. The Reach-born gods carry knowledge of the Axioms they cannot read, and the approach to the center harms them. Most gods have no clear sense of the Core beneath the Radiant Citadel.

What Vecna saw — what he kept seeing, the longer he looked — was an opportunity to reach past godhood. If the universe's emergency-archival system runs on Zeniths and Monoliths, and if a being could choose which Monoliths get written and where, that being could compose with the universe's own ink. They would become more than a god. They would become structural.

That is what Vecna is trying to do. Not become a bigger god. Become a condition.

What he is actually after Beneath the inscription is the Reach. The Core stands above the remains of the Reach world at the center of the cosmos, and it holds the record of how Axiom contact was attempted — the only account of how this cosmos began. Vecna is the god of secrets, and that is the one secret larger than all the others. The figure is how he reaches it. The rewriting is the price of admission. His spell only gets one cast.

The First Seeker DM understanding

Vecna's method was done to him before he did it to anyone else.

As a boy on Oerth, bound to an order of wizards as a bootblack and then a scribe, he educated himself at night in his masters' library. During those study sessions he first heard a voice — a whisper from another world, promising him revenge if he gave in to the hatred in him. After he massacred the order, the same voice urged him to record every thought he had, and he began the Book of Vile Darkness. The voice directed him for centuries, through the conquest, the lichdom, and the world-hopping. One account of Kas's betrayal holds that the voice came to fear Vecna and began whispering to Kas instead.

Vecna was taught how to look, by a voice, once. Everything he does to the Seekers is a repetition of the only thing that ever worked on him.

The voice that taught him It has never been identified. It spoke to him across centuries, and one account holds that it turned to Kas when it came to fear what Vecna had become. It sits in his history, unnamed, and the cosmology has a floor beneath the floor whenever the campaign wants one.

What the working requires

The figure is drawn across the cosmos with Monoliths as its anchors. Reaching a position takes two separate pieces of work, in order, and a third for the worlds that carry the figure's strokes.

A Monolith has to exist Canon

A Monolith comes from a Zenith and from nothing else. Where a Monolith already stands, Vecna has a position he can reach. Where none stands, a world must be driven across its threshold.

Monoliths persist after their Zenith resolves because they are archives, not responses in progress. The Monolith is the permanent record of that Zenith, and the cosmos is old enough that most of the Monoliths standing were raised long before Vecna existed. Most of his work is claiming what already stands. Inducing new Zeniths is what he does where nothing stands.

The world beneath has to be marked Canon

A Monolith relays to the Core through the world beneath it. The Monolith itself is beyond his reach to alter; the world is not.

Marking is work done at a world's resonance sites — the places where the world's nature runs closest to the surface. The work takes weeks, requires sustained ground access, and leaves a signature inside the Monolith's relay. An unmarked Monolith is a position he can reach. A marked one is a position he holds.

Marking requires no artifact and does not touch the world's heart. It is laborious, conspicuous if anyone is watching, and the most common kind of operation the crew will stumble into.

Link-bearing worlds have to be opened Canon

The figure's strokes between nodes are not drawn in the sky. They are heart-links, and a heart-link requires both worlds to be opened.

Every world has a heart — its accumulated self — reachable through a single hidden Aperture. Finding an Aperture takes an artifact or generations of scholarship. Passing through it and reaching the heart is a descent through the world's own interior, shaped by what that world is. Opening unfolds the world so that its heart is reachable from outside and its yearning becomes active.

The coupling A heart can only be opened where a Monolith stands. Without one, the Aperture stays folded no matter who is looking. This is the single rule that binds the two halves of the campaign together: the Zenith track and the invasion track are the same track, approached from opposite ends.

Two opened worlds can link, heart to heart — permanently, in both directions, and faster than any crossing.

The ladder Fixed
  • World. Marked. The Monolith over it carries his signature.
  • Node. Three marked worlds in one system. Below three, the system holds nothing.
  • Sphere. Three nodes make a sphere ready.
  • The triangle. A sphere's three nodes link to one another, closing a triangle inside the sphere. This requires one opened world in each node.
  • The cross-weave. Two of those three nodes reach outward, each to one node in each of the two spheres that are not its neighbours. A node never reaches an adjacent sphere.
  • The Core. The third node of each sphere reaches nowhere else. It is reserved for that sphere's link to the Core, which is the final stroke, and nothing can be added after it.

Five spheres, three nodes each, three marked worlds per node: forty-five marked worlds, fifteen nodes, fifteen opened worlds at the floor, plus the reserved Core links. Every marked world needs an operation on the ground and a Monolith standing overhead. Every opened world needs a descent.

Why he needs lieutenants A node's links run to the two spheres furthest from it, so closing one requires work in three places at once, on opposite sides of the Flow, by people who cannot easily reach each other. Vecna cannot hand-run that. His immediate followers hold the cross-sphere pairings, and each of them knows a piece of the geometry. Take one and you learn which two spheres their nodes bind.

This is why the figure needs a lot and not a handful. Surplus is what makes a loss survivable: a fourth node in a sphere, or a fourth marked world in a node, means a destroyed Monolith costs a substitution instead of the rung.

The Core is last because it closes the figure. Vecna is a later god, so the approach to the center harms him far less than it would a Reach-born god. He does not know what is down there. He knows the center is the thinnest point in the cosmology, that his figure has to pass through it, and he suspects the record of the Reach is there.

What he still needs Open
  • Residue pieces, for reach. They tell him which worlds can be driven to a Zenith and what a Monolith there would serve. A.T.L.A.S.'s Boundary piece points toward its four siblings.
  • A means of opening worlds at scale. Fifteen descents is more work than any agent he has can perform. He does not do it himself, and the only party in the cosmos doing it is the Vth'orama, who believe they are doing it for their own reasons.
  • Zeniths driven where nothing stands. The expensive option, used where a position has no Monolith and no prospect of one.
  • A way into the Core. Unsolved.

Dual attack — the spell and the stress DM understanding

Vecna's plan is two simultaneous things, not one. Both moves are powered by the same work, which is why he doesn't need separate operations for them.

The spell — drawing the figure
The visible plan. He selects Monolith positions across the cosmos to compose an arcane figure that, when complete, functions as a cosmic spell. When the spell completes, it routes Continuity through him. This is the part the crew can see, eventually, by tracking artifacts, charts, and Zenith patterns.
The stress — pushing Convergence toward Fracture
The invisible plan, running in the background. Every Zenith he engineers, every Monolith that comes online under his influence, places additional strain on Convergence — the Axiom producing the Zeniths. The strain accumulates within the Continuity equilibrium that Convergence helps sustain. If enough Monoliths come online under his influence, Convergence approaches Axiom Fracture. The Shock-and-Unraveling that follows would force Continuity to find a new equilibrium — and he would be positioned, by virtue of the figure now standing, to be part of the replacement.

The two attacks reinforce each other. Every act of drawing the figure also stresses Convergence; every Zenith engineered to add a point also feeds the Fracture. He cannot do one without doing the other. That's not a coincidence — it's why he chose this method specifically.

Why this is the right plan, from his perspective Monolith-based exploitation is one path to Continuum-scale influence; other paths exist. Vecna found this one because it is the only known method where the spell and the structural attack are the same work. Other Axiom-fracture paths would require him to design two operations and run them in parallel. This one runs both off the same set of moves.

The crew's actual epistemic work is harder than it looks. They aren't tracking a villain who is doing two things — they're tracking a villain whose every visible act has an invisible structural consequence. Even delaying his next move buys Continuity time to recover from the last stress increment. Even witnessing his moves and reporting them allows the Watchers to start managing the strain. Slowing him is meaningful before they understand what he's doing.

Mechanism — selection, not creation
A Monolith is a point the universe has already drawn or is going to draw
It marks a place where a Zenith happened or is about to happen. Vecna does not create Monoliths; nobody does. He selects positions where they exist (standing Monoliths from past Zeniths, sometimes centuries old) or where they could be made to exist at the right moment. Selection happens across time as well as space.

His involvement in any given Zenith falls into three tiers.

TierWhat it isExample
Taught A Seeker chapter exists on the world, carrying the method he seeded. Most chapters produce nothing. A few, over decades or centuries, produce the conditions for a threshold. Most of the cosmos's chapters.
Nudged He acts on a specific world — a vision, a dream, a text placed where it will be found, a doctrine introduced to a movement already forming. Ranges from a quick nudge on a world already approaching its threshold to centuries of long-arc seeding. Aethris (timed to cross). Fonains (encouraged, then lost control).
Untouched The Zenith has nothing to do with him. Civilizations reach apex, rituals reach saturation, magic systems peak, the Order manages what it can. The vast majority of Continuum activity.
The crew's actual epistemic work If the crew investigates ten Zeniths, eight of them are not his — and figuring out which is which is most of the work. Their job is never "stop Zeniths." It is tell which ones he is reaching for. A Watcher might be present at an unrelated Zenith trying to save it. The Vth'orama might be present at another waiting for it to bloom. From the surface, both look like cosmic events with strangers attending.

Not every seed works. Some cults collapse. Some artifacts are destroyed. Some civilizations refuse the path. The figure must be revisable. When a planted Zenith doesn't materialize where he needed it, he redesigns the inscription — finds a new figure that uses different points, or recovers a near-failure into a different working. The shape of the spell is fixed at any given moment; the route to it is iterative. He has been at this a long time.

Fonains forced the largest revision Vecna encouraged the Zenith on Fonains because he wanted a Monolith there. He did not know that Tharizdun had corrupted the Monolith. Lola's Boys destroyed it — the only Monolith on record to be destroyed. What that cost him was one world, not the system. A node needs three marked worlds, and SYS-04 is short by the one they took: he can mark another world there, or move the node to another system in the sphere. Either way it is recoverable, and either way it costs him time he had already spent. Before that he treated a standing Monolith as a fixed asset and worked a short list of positions. Losing one proved the list was fragile. He now works for surplus.
The figure has to be specific
He has chosen one shape. Only one. The marks have to land in specific places in three dimensions; near-misses don't work. This is why he needs certain Monoliths in certain places — both standing ones to claim and approaching ones to nudge.

The Seekers Locked

Vecna's following. "Seekers" is a convenience of this document — each chapter calls itself something of its own, and every institution in the cosmos reads them as unrelated. Full faction treatment in Factions.

A chapter is a group on a single world pursuing a secret it believes was kept from it. The secret is local and specific: a drowned city, a lost language, the fate of a vanished order, where their people came from, what lies past death.

The four beliefs

Every chapter holds these, however it words them.

  1. Something was taken from us. There was a time when more was known.
  2. It can be recovered. Not invented — recovered. The knowledge still exists somewhere.
  3. Recovery requires giving something up. Comfort, a name, a body, a self.
  4. The voice is proof. Someone among us heard something, and that is how we know we are right.

The founding story The through-line

Every chapter has one, and they are structurally identical across worlds that have never touched. Someone was reaching. Something answered. What it gave them was a method rather than an answer — it taught them how to look.

A saint who received a discipline. A scholar who dreamed a procedure. An ancestor who came back from somewhere and taught the family how to listen. A text found in a ruin, written as instruction rather than doctrine.

No chapter's founding story includes being given what they were searching for. A local revelation gives answers. This one only ever gave a method — which is the signature of something that wanted the searching to happen and did not care where it led.

The voice

Anyone who has heard it describes the same thing: quiet, close, as though the speaker were beside them. It said one thing. It did not repeat itself, and it did not answer a follow-up.

The description is what connects the chapters. Different worlds, different names, same texture. A party that has collected three accounts has the pattern whether or not anyone explains it to them.

What they call him

Each world coins its own: the Unasked, the Unanswered, the One Taught Before Us, the Elder Student, the One Who Was Told First. The most widespread rendering is the First Seeker — a title the chapters read as modesty, meaning only that he asked before any of them did.

It is literally true. No chapter knows the name Vecna. A few of the oldest hold a word in a dead language that is a corruption of it.

Practice

Varies by chapter. Common elements, inherited rather than coordinated:

  • Nothing is held whole. Records are deliberately partial and distributed. No one person holds all of anything.
  • Knowledge is traded, never given. A chapter answers a question only in exchange for one.
  • Cells. Members know their immediate contacts and little else. This is why chapters on the same world can exist without knowing about each other, and why no institution has ever assembled the picture.
  • Failures are recorded. Unusual enough to be a real tell; most traditions record successes.
The exception The Book of Vile Darkness is the record of Vecna's every thought and dream, begun at the voice's urging and carried on his person — he shaped his lich form to encompass it. It is the one complete account in existence, and the practice of keeping nothing whole exists so that nobody else ever assembles one.

Marks

Each chapter's mark depicts the thing it is looking for, drawn so that the subject itself is absent and only its surroundings are rendered. The subject is the hole in the middle.

  • The water and the seabed, with the tower blank
  • A line of text with one character's space left untouched
  • A crest's border and field, with the device missing
  • A road trailing into unmarked ground

Carved as a cut through the stone. Inked as the one patch of bare page. Woven as a gap in the pattern. Cast as a pierced disc.

The marks share no shape. They share a technique. The first time the crew sees one it is strange art; the second time, on another world, it is a question; after that they start looking for the hole in things.

Held in reserve A chapter somewhere has filled its hole. Something is drawn in the empty space. Whatever they put there is either a real answer or a catastrophic wrong guess, and either way it is the most dangerous page in the campaign.

The Vth'orama Locked

N'ghathrod's illithid colony — a Seeker chapter, the largest in the cosmos and the only one that travels. Full treatment in Factions. What matters to the Remaking is below.

The load-bearing fact The Vth'orama are the only party in the cosmos opening worlds at scale. Vecna's link layer is being built, world by world, by someone who believes he is building an empire and who has never heard of the figure, the Axioms, or Vecna.

N'ghathrod wants the lattice as travel and colony infrastructure — an invasion that arrives without crossing wildspace. He believes he serves an elder brain. He has never been given an order.

The dependency runs both ways:

  • He needs Zeniths, because a heart cannot be opened without a Monolith. So he coordinates with planet-bound chapters: they do what they do on their world, and when it crosses, he comes.
  • Vecna needs opened worlds, and has no other means of getting them.
Saturation Zeniths are the cheap half of the work; Apertures are rare and slow to find. The strategy is to push as many worlds over as possible so that the few Apertures found already have a Monolith waiting. The visible symptom is Zeniths occurring far more often than they should, across unconnected worlds — an escalation the crew can observe for a long time before anyone can explain it.

The cost

Everything the Vth'orama does costs tadpoles, and N'ghathrod's own production runs on a slow cycle. A person costs one. Something enormous costs many. A crate of tadpoles in transit is a strategic target — destroying one costs him months of placements, and it is a win the crew can measure long before they can touch him.

The Caelestis operation Locked

Two operations ran against Caelestis in the same window, by two parties, one of whom does not know the other exists.

Caelestis is teleport-sealed (see Realms → Caelestis → Security); no one teleports in, out, or within the station. Anything wanted from the Brig had to be reached physically, and reaching it required first understanding the station: routes, schedules, courier protocols, response patterns, which shifts overlap, where security thins, who has clearance to know what.

Vocath's operation Locked

A mercane with a commercial and personal grudge. A merchant, working his own quarrel, by proxy. His aim is to disgrace Mirt enough to prise him loose from the school's protection so someone else finishes him. The school is Mirt's shield; Vocath needs to wreck Mirt's standing without confronting him directly.

The thefts, the sabotage plates, the leverage on Miken and the disc-marked crate cache at the Sky Dock are all his, and all real. The eye-in-a-wheel is his own merchant mark — it appears on his goods wherever they move, which is why it turns up on the rod, on the Sky Dock crate, and at the Cairn Station engagement.

He takes no part in the assault on Caelestis. He is a merchant with a grudge, and his contribution is noise.

The arrangement Locked

Zhauveth — one of the Enna Vth'ghathu — approached Vocath roughly a year before Arc I. The offer was an asset inside Caelestis, a place Vocath had been unable to reach for years.

Vocath understood that he was dealing with a mind flayer offering a thralled agent. Ordinary illithid work: repellent, effective, and no concern of his. He asked no further questions.

The price was a Caelestis crew, taken at a time and place Vocath could arrange. He read that as payment in bodies, which is what illithids always want.

Cairn Station Locked

Vocath's people ambushed a Caelestis crew running near Cairn Station. In the engagement Clint Dashforest went overboard and psychic wind carried him out of the system entirely. Most of the crew did not return.

Joffrey was taken — a tadpole of N'ghathrod's, implanted, with its directive already taught to it before it was carried. A handful of survivors were released so that there would be a story, and they came home with him.

The account held. Joffrey was decorated, posted to Caelestis, and rose on genuine merit. The taking matured over the following year, which is why nothing was visibly wrong about him for most of it.

What Vocath received: an agent inside Caelestis who reports to him, accurately and usefully. What Vocath did not receive: anything. Joffrey is himself. He thinks, speaks and works as he always did, and everything about him reads true. His allegiance runs to N'ghathrod.

The run Locked

The Brig's location and access protocols are themselves classified. Surface reconnaissance was insufficient, so the operative planted tracking material on a known artifact-bound shipment and watched where it went. Vocath's cover thefts and the bribed dock seam created the inventory turbulence that made anomalous handling normal.

Jeffrey is the dock seam — bribed, not taken, and with no idea what his friend is. Blip is not complicit.

By graduation, Joffrey has the physical map of how to reach the Brig and how to leave the station afterward. Vocath's chaos provides the diversion. Joffrey runs.

Two thieves in the same chaos Vocath's people grab visible grudge-loot — Ryeback's pistol, restricted supplies, the disc-marked crates. Joffrey is doing something else entirely. Neither of them knows what the other is for.

Artifact — A.T.L.A.S.

A brass-and-glass projection device with a paracausal core — a piece of Reach residue biased toward Boundary. Currently held in the Brig.

The housing is a working instrument. Activated near a planetary body, it charts the world and surfaces what the surface conceals: water at depth, ore, voids, structures, the oldest ground. Caelestis has catalogued it as a rare navigational instrument. They know it does something and have not committed institutional resources to understanding what.

Three readers, three depths

A.T.L.A.S.'s readings carry information at three cosmological depths. What each reader extracts depends on what they bring.

  • The crew read at surface. Each world they take it to reveals adventure hooks — where the world's secret lives, often a hard dungeon — and a real navigational advantage. Immediate, genuine value. This is what the device "is" to them.
  • N'ghathrod reads for Apertures. The device shows him where a world's heart can be entered. Without it he surveys by hand: slow, expensive, and frequently wrong. This is the single thing that would most accelerate the Vth'orama.
  • Vecna reads structurally. The readings resolve into which worlds resonate with which — the lattice's connectivity itself. With it he can stop marking broadly and start targeting the optimal few. He doesn't need the device. He needs the piece.

Same data, three reads. The depths are the artifact's property; the named parties are this campaign's readers.

The literacy asymmetry: if the crew ends up holding A.T.L.A.S., they have a real and usable tool immediately. They do not get the Aperture read or the structural read. What they have is the surface, and that surface is genuinely valuable. The decode of what the device actually is happens slowly, voyage by voyage, as they accumulate readings and edge cases.

For Vecna, the housing is disposable. The Boundary piece is the prize. If it leaves Caelestis, the brass is eventually pried open and the case discarded. A crew recovering A.T.L.A.S. after would find a broken device with the piece gone — and would understand, retroactively, what the whole operation was about.

The race Running pattern

A directional pattern to thread through Arc II, not a railroad. A.T.L.A.S.'s readings produce a competitive geography — the crew sees adventure hooks at each indicated world; the Vth'orama see the same sites as Apertures. They reach for the same physical destinations for different reasons. Both parties are time-pressured.

Crew arrives first
Untouched site. They explore on their own terms. Whatever they do becomes the prior condition the colony's later arrival must work around.
Crew arrives during
Direct conflict at an Aperture or a resonance site. The crew can interrupt, contest, capture, or be driven off. The highest-stakes version of the encounter.
Crew arrives second
The work is done. They can investigate the wake, sometimes undo work that is still fresh, sometimes only learn from what is left. Over time this is how the crew realizes something is racing them.
How the colony finds the same places Slowly, by hand. Scholars and archivists taken for what they know. Surveys dug at a world's deepest accessible points, frequently in the wrong place and years ahead of any need. The Anadia well is one of these — a world with neither Monolith nor Zenith, surveyed and abandoned when the dig came up empty. The bodies in the side cut were the labour.
Inspiration The Tomb Takers in the Aeor ruins. Not a faction the party opposed in the abstract — a faction with overlapping physical objectives at specific sites, with time pressure on both sides. The competitive pressure produces texture neither side generates alone.

Failure state Fixed

Vecna completes the figure. Every required Monolith stands or has been responded into being where the figure needs it; the points are linked into a single channel; the final Zenith is timed. The cumulative strain triggers the Axiom Fracture he has been working toward — Convergence cracks, the Shock cascades, and the Continuum begins to find new equilibrium around the only structural principle still standing in the vacancy: him. The finished spell routes all of reality through him. He becomes the answer to who gets to be real.

Failure state has a name If the figure completes, the world becomes the published apocalypse (Vecna's Ritual of Remaking). This is not a module we might run — it is the shape of the world if the crew does nothing, bearing down as the future. Every point they fail to contest moves the world one mark closer to that book. Late-arc material lives in The Spine → Act 3.

Node ladder Fixed order

The only villain-planning that exists in advance. Order is fixed; timing is flexible. Crew enters between nodes 1 and 2. Arc II lives in nodes 2 and 3.

The crew can delay, witness, or complicate any node. They cannot make the work stop progressing toward the next one. You know the order; you never know the exact when. Arc II is a long runway — the channel doesn't form until late.

Planning method Standing rule

The plan is held at three resolutions, and only three. This is the discipline that keeps the living world from collapsing into a flowchart.

The End (D) — fixed
Never changes. The gravity everything falls toward.
The Nodes — fixed in sequence, flexible in timing
The load-bearing steps above. The crew bends when, never whether.
The Next Immediate Step — the only thing planned in detail
Where the work is reaching right now, concrete enough to run. The moment it resolves — with or without the party — author the next immediate step in response to what actually happened.

Everything between the nodes — couriers, side-deals, small moves — is improvised at the table and written down only after it happens, as history. Never pre-planned, because the party and the world are what fill that space.

The shape, always A is the plan. B happened (the party acted). C is the response. They still have to reach D. Maintain three things only: the goal, the ordered node list, and one index card that says "what is moving this minute." After each voyage, throw away the card and write the next one from what the crew did. Log the result in the Node Log above.

Fork — the Brig heist sets the tempo

The Fork is the heist outcome. During graduation chaos, with security diverted by the surface operation, Joffrey runs the Brig. He reaches the vault; he gets A.T.L.A.S.; he has only the Sky Dock chaos to extract through, because he can't teleport out. The Brig itself is dangerous — wards, constructs, automated responses — and he comes out haggard, wounded, half-broken, having burned through every contingency just to get this far.

What the body shows If Joffrey is captured or killed: chitin beneath the skin along the spine and the back of the skull, thin and recent. The jaw and hard palate wrong underneath. The brain intact — which makes no sense to anyone who knows what illithids do to a host. His mind cannot be read. He says one word: Oramu.

The crew is the variable. Three outcomes, all interesting, none "wrong":

State A — Thief escapes Operation completed

The crew arrives too late, or doesn't intercept, or loses the engagement. Joffrey — barely — makes it to the Sky Dock and onto a ship. A.T.L.A.S. leaves Caelestis. Caelestis knows something major happened (you don't quietly empty the Brig), but they don't know what was taken or where it went.

Tone
The door's already been kicked in by someone else. Who was here before us, and where did they go?
Arc II is a CHASE
The crew tracks A.T.L.A.S. across Wildspace, arriving at sites after the colony has been there — readings already taken, Apertures already found, worlds already opened. They're behind. They feel it.
Caelestis posture
Mirt's standing takes a meaningful hit — the Brig was breached on his watch, during his crew's graduation. Vocath got exactly what he wanted, by accident. Mortgaged trust, useful for future Bridge tensions.

State B1 — Crew returns A.T.L.A.S. Operation foiled

The crew intercepts during extraction. He is already badly wounded; if pressed hard, he breaks. They take A.T.L.A.S. and hand it back to Caelestis officials. The academy now knows A.T.L.A.S. exists as a target — its institutional understanding of its own collection just changed.

Tone
The crew won and behaved as Driftwardens should. Caelestis owes them a debt; Mirt's standing is enhanced; the Brig's curators start asking why someone went through that much trouble for this device.
Arc II is a CONTEST with institutional backing
The crew can request access to study A.T.L.A.S. later. The academy quietly starts its own investigation; the crew may be looped in or may not.
Alive vs. dead
A living Joffrey is a thread into protocols, contacts and recognition codes — and an institutional crisis, because he is a decorated officer whose mind cannot be read. A dead one leaves a body that raises the same questions with none of the answers.

State B2 — Crew keeps A.T.L.A.S. Maximum engagement

Same engagement, different decision. The crew takes A.T.L.A.S. and doesn't hand it back. They might justify it ("they knew Caelestis had it; Caelestis can't keep it safe"); they might just feel a pull toward keeping the thing they fought for.

Tone
An act of institutional theft, even if morally defensible. Caelestis may or may not know. If they know, this becomes a relationship problem. If they don't, the crew sits on a secret of their own.
Arc II is the LITERACY DECODE
Voyage by voyage, edge case by edge case, the crew learns the device. Each world they take it to feeds the pattern. Eventually — Arc II late, possibly Arc III — they see enough to recognize what it is reading. They earn the meaning.
They are now the target
Future encounters with the Vth'orama may be specifically about recovery. N'ghathrod does not forget, and he does not let go.

Either way the spine holds. The work continues regardless — slower without the device, faster with it. State A accelerates the colony; States B1 and B2 hold them where they were. The crew bends the tempo, not the direction. Lock the resulting state in Current State once graduation runs.

If the crew misses the engagement entirely If the heist runs and the crew is somewhere else — saving a wing, fighting through Vocath's chaos, distracted by an academy emergency — A.T.L.A.S. escapes by default. State A. They learn later, possibly much later, that something was taken from the Brig that night. The Brig was never something cadets were supposed to know about, so this discovery is also an institutional reveal.
