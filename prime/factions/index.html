Order of the All-Father

An interworld organization that interprets the influence of the All-Father and acts to preserve Continuity where possible. Recruits from many worlds and cultures; members include mystics, scholars, spelljammers, operatives, diplomats, ritual specialists, and planetary wardens. Operates with two field arms: the Watchers (sanctioned) and Rogue Watchers (heterodox).

Watchers

The Order's sanctioned field arm. A known partner organization to the Fleet. Authority is divine-by-belief: members hold that the All-Father charged them, and carry themselves with the bearing of a priesthood.

Function: observe, report on, and where necessary intervene in worlds approaching dangerous cosmological thresholds. Their mandate is preservation of Continuity, not universal rescue — a distinction the order takes seriously.

Operations rely on the Fleet for transport and reach; the Fleet relies on the order for moral mandate and divine cover.

Rogue Watchers

Watchers who do not follow the Order's protocols as closely as it would prefer. They operate on their own judgment — sometimes intervening where doctrine would not, sometimes failing to intervene where doctrine would expect, sometimes taking the work in a different direction than they were sent. A range, not a category. Some are nearly indistinguishable from sanctioned Watchers; some are openly heterodox.

The Order tolerates them with varying degrees of patience depending on the individual and the era. A Rogue Watcher who saves a world by acting outside doctrine is quietly celebrated; one who violates a precept and creates downstream harm is censured. The line between principled disobedience and dangerous overreach is not always clear to either side.

They appear in the world as local protectors, unorthodox interventionists, or quietly excommunicated former colleagues of sanctioned Watchers. Most worlds that encounter one cannot distinguish a Watcher from a Rogue Watcher.

Caelestis Academy & Fleet

A self-made, non-governmental organization headquartered at the Caelestis station in Realmspace. The Academy is the recruitment and training arm; the Fleet is the operational body that grew from it. Authority is self-bestowed by reputation rather than granted by any council of worlds.

Operations are for-hire and frequently covert. Most worlds do not know that Wildspace exists; the Fleet blends in, deals with the few locals who are read in, and leaves without rewriting a civilization's reality. Contracts are load-bearing — they are how the Fleet sustains itself.

Cadet graduates are called Driftwardens — the in-world term for the operational corps.

The Bridge

The Fleet's senior command. Members oversee policy, training, funding, logistics, security, and mission assignment. Not omniscient; not always united. Individual officers vary in how much they understand about cosmological stakes, and in how they read any given incident.

What Mirt is actually working on

He holds Above Bridge clearance, so he has read WM-002 and FR-016. He recognizes the eye in the wheel on sight. It is Vocath's house mark, and he has spent years on the other side of a commercial war with the man who uses it. He knows an eye-in-a-wheel craft mauled a Fleet relief vessel on the Cairn approach, he knows the same mark is on half the incidents at his own station now, and he knows exactly whose it is.

What he has is certainty the Bridge cannot act on. FR-016's cross-reference to the registry returned no match, because a mercane's private house mark was never in it. Mirt knows the mark the way a rival knows a rival, which is to say personally, which is to say it is worth nothing in a filing. Naming Vocath in the record would read as a headmaster using Fleet intelligence to prosecute his own feud, and it would put the Academy's protection of him in play the moment someone decided to look at it that way.

So he backed the closure instead. Keeping Fleet vessels off that bearing stops anyone else dying out there while he handles Vocath himself, by means that do not require proof. He holds the position that the sector is lethal and unexplained, and he holds it in full knowledge that it is neither. The cost he accepts for this: nobody will find out what is actually at Cairn, and nobody will look twice at who came home from it.

What he carries is that he could have ended Vocath years ago and chose the slower route. He had openings, took the commercial ones, and let it run because it was only ever money. A Fleet crew died on that bearing, and he has sat with the arithmetic of that since the file crossed his desk. It is why he moves on Vocath personally now rather than through channels.

What he will still not reach for is the next step. The Crow's survivors came home, were debriefed, and were cleared. One of them is on his station, decorated, and good at the job. Mirt is certain about Vocath, which is precisely what keeps him from looking at Joffrey — the explanation he already has accounts for the facts he already has. The Seekers

Chapters on dozens of worlds, each pursuing a secret it believes was kept from it. "Seekers" is this document's convenience. No chapter calls itself that. No two chapters share a name, a symbol, a practice or a god. No institution in the cosmos recognizes them as one thing, and neither do they.

What they share is an origin. Each was taught, once, by a voice — how to look, never what would be found. That voice was Vecna. Structural detail in the Remaking.

What a chapter usually is A chapter is whatever its world allows: a scholarly order inside a legitimate institution, a mourning tradition, an established state faith, a persecuted heresy, a reform movement with a forgotten phrase in its charter, or a custom whose meaning was lost generations ago. Some are benign and doing real work. Some are dangerous for reasons that have nothing to do with Vecna. The first chapter the crew meets should be a good one.

What every chapter holds
  1. Something was taken from us. There was a time when more was known.
  2. It can be recovered. Not invented — recovered. The knowledge still exists somewhere.
  3. Recovery requires giving something up. Comfort, a name, a body, a self.
  4. The voice is proof. Someone among us heard something, and that is how we know we are right.

The founding story. Structurally identical across worlds that have never touched. Someone was reaching. Something answered. What it gave them was a method rather than an answer. A saint who received a discipline; a scholar who dreamed a procedure; an ancestor who came back and taught the family how to listen; a text found in a ruin, written as instruction rather than doctrine.

No chapter's founding story includes being given what they were searching for.

The voice. Anyone who has heard it describes the same thing: quiet, close, as though the speaker were beside them. It said one thing. It did not repeat itself and it did not answer a follow-up. This is what connects the chapters — the account of the voice, rather than any symbol, name or rite they hold in common. A party with three accounts has the pattern.

What they call him. Each world coins its own: the Unasked, the Unanswered, the One Taught Before Us, the Elder Student, the One Who Was Told First. The most widespread is the First Seeker — read as modesty, meaning only that he asked before any of them did. It is literally true. No chapter knows the name Vecna.

The eldest chapter The Restorers are the first of these, and the only one that outgrew its own world and became an order in its own right. They hold the four claims, carry the founding account as history, and cut their mark by the same technique. Their reading of the resemblance is that the other chapters are imitations of their work.

Practice
  • Nothing is held whole. Records are deliberately partial and distributed. No one person holds all of anything.
  • Knowledge is traded, never given. A chapter answers a question only in exchange for one. This is the most reliable way to deal with them and the most expensive.
  • Cells. Members know their immediate contacts and little else. Two chapters on the same world can exist without knowing about each other.
  • Failures are recorded. Unusual enough to be a tell; most traditions record successes.

Marks. Each chapter's mark depicts the thing it is looking for, drawn so that the subject is absent and only its surroundings are rendered — the subject is the hole in the middle. The water and the seabed with the tower blank. A line of text with one character's space untouched. A crest's border and field with the device missing. Carved as a cut through the stone, inked as the one patch of bare page, woven as a gap in the pattern, cast as a pierced disc.

The marks share no shape. They share a technique.

Hessa Dun's uncle Planted, Arc I

A cadet, third intake, with four crates of family effects arriving at the Sea Dock and nowhere to put them. The crates belong to an uncle — alive, travelling, odd, and the sort of relative nobody in the family takes seriously. Books, ledgers, and bundles of correspondence.

The letters are between the uncle and people on other worlds, carried by whatever ships would take them, going back decades. The correspondents are strangers who write to each other about what they are each looking into.

Hessa explains it as a family habit: her uncle has a way of asking about things that he was taught, and that he taught her as a child. She has been meaning to write back to one of the correspondents and has not.

The point of it The crew's first contact with the Seekers should be a half-day of moving crates for a decent person, with nothing at stake and nothing offered. What they can take away is the letters, freely given, because Hessa thinks they are family papers. If they skip it, the crates still get moved and the uncle still exists.

The Vth'orama

An illithid colony under N'ghathrod, living aboard a taken astral dreadnought and working across Realmspace and beyond. The name means the whole of the passage — they are named for the ship, because the ship is what they are. It covers everyone aboard: N'ghathrod, the bearers, the unthinned, the taken, the thralls and their children.

They are a Seeker chapter — the largest in the cosmos and the only one that travels. They do not know it. They believe they serve an elder brain.

What they want: a home. Refused by Glyth after N'ghathrod returned with illithids the colony judged unfit, they are building their own — a lattice of opened, linked worlds a colony can be seeded into without crossing wildspace. Full plan in the Remaking.

What nobody knows The Vth'orama are the only party in the cosmos opening worlds at scale. Every link they build is a stroke of Vecna's figure. N'ghathrod has never heard the name.

The taking Locked

N'ghathrod was captured and experimented on aboard Thessalar's planar craft, in the presence of the seventh piece of the Rod of Seven Parts. The piece is Convergence residue, and its associated working is simulacrum. What it changed was his reproduction.

His tadpoles are copies of himself. Implanted into a host, such a tadpole arrests rather than consuming: the host persists, and the copy sits alongside them.

A taken person — an n'ghathu — therefore:

  • Keeps their memories, skills, personality, relationships and competence. They are still themselves.
  • Grows illithid chitin beneath the skin over years, hidden, along the spine and the back of the skull.
  • Gains the colony bond, and knows exactly what they belong to.
  • Has their allegiance run to N'ghathrod. When they must choose, they choose him.
  • Carries an innate mind blank. They cannot be read — which is itself the single most reliable tell.
It is not experienced as a taking From the inside it is a gift, freely given and gladly taken. Many of the kesh were offered it and accepted — the outcast, the discarded, the ones with nothing, told they could belong and still be themselves. The colony's word for them is a kinship term.

Freeing one requires removing the tadpole, and only works on someone who knows and objects. For the willing there is nothing to remove them from. Otherwise the only release is N'ghathrod's death, which ends every n'ghathu at once.

Tadpoles are the cost of everything. He grows them on a slow cycle; a person costs one, something enormous costs many. Anyone can carry and implant one, so couriers and brine casks move constantly — and a crate destroyed in transit costs him months of placements.

Tenure Encounter dial

An n'ghathu develops N'ghathrod's faculties over time, because the copy is becoming more fully itself. How long ago someone was taken is the whole difficulty knob, and it is diegetic.

Three lines of development, so no two are alike. The Seer — detect thoughts, then telepathy, then true seeing. The Voice — suggestion, then charm, then dominate. The Flesh — chitin thickens, reach extends, eventually tentacles.

Nothing is Lost When an n'ghathu dies, N'ghathrod receives what it knew — including everything it learned about whoever killed it. Every taken creature is a tripwire. A party that works this out has to start solving dungeons without killing the guards.

As a template: apply to any stat block. Keep everything the creature had; add chitin (reduce b/p/s damage by 3, or 5 at long tenure), mind blank, the tenure's faculties, and Nothing is Lost. CR +1 short tenure, +2 to +3 long. It works on beasts as readily as people — a taken guard animal fights tactically far beyond its intelligence, which is the tell.

Enna Vth'ghathu — the five

Five illithids, taken a century ago, each carrying a Hold — a portion of N'ghathrod himself. All five offered themselves — the three unthinned in gratitude, because he gave them what bad rearing denied them; the two bearers in loyalty, because they could have stayed on Glyth and chose him.

He speaks through them at will. He does not drive them. They are still themselves, and they are also him, and the colony has no difficulty holding both ideas at once.

Killing one ends that Hold permanently. He cannot replace it without spending more of himself than he has left, and the colony loses the function entirely. The party will not know they have done this; they will think they killed a mind flayer.

Build them between CR 12 and 16, each lopsided. None of them is a balanced encounter.

Beliefs

They defected from Glyth, not from being illithids. Every one of them was reared in illithid culture, and the things that culture put in them are load-bearing rather than decorative. A crew hoping to talk the colony out of its project will run into these, and none of them is an argument that can be won.

What they keptHow it shows
Union with a greater mind is the proper end of a life. Absorption into an elder brain is what service is repaid with. It is why all five enna offered themselves rather than being compelled, and why the colony has no difficulty with someone being themselves and also him.
A colony must have a centre. N'ghathrod being the ship is not strange to them. It is correct. A colony without a centre is the thing they fled, and nothing in their experience suggests another shape is possible.
The unculled are an obscenity. Held alongside the fact that most of the colony is unculled, and three of the five enna are. They use Glyth's insult for themselves. This is the one inherited belief that cuts against them, and they have never resolved it — they have only outlived the people who said it.
Thralls are material. No one aboard can be argued out of this. It is why the captive trade continues without discomfort, and why the bearers' grievance is about allocation rather than ethics: they object to a body being wasted, never to the taking of it.
Qualith is the only real writing. Their records are untranslatable by design rather than by accident, and they assume anything written otherwise is beneath reading.
Where the leverage actually is Three of those five are closed doors. The contradiction about the unculled is the one that is open, because they are living on both sides of it — and the bearers are the people in whom it is loudest. A crew that understands the colony's history can press on a wound the colony inflicted on itself, rather than arguing a position no illithid has ever abandoned.

Vth'merru — the bearers

Properly-raised illithids who left Glyth with him. Two or three remain, very old, and they are the only reason the colony has a future: they lay into the pools and oversee ordinary ceremorphosis. N'ghathrod can make himself, in pieces, across worlds. He cannot make a colony.

This is why the captive trade exists — the raiding, the neogi arrangements, the bodies. Every captive is an allocation decision, and the bearers watch bodies that should have become illithids receive one of his tadpoles instead and walk away still looking like a person. To them that is a child wasted on a disguise.

The best people aboard to reach Untaken, uncopied, a century of grievance, and old enough to remember Glyth and to have known him before the Rod. They never leave Vth'oramu. They are tended, protected, and quietly disagreed with. Open: whether any bearer has ever asked to be taken and been refused.

Vth'ulhan — the unthinned

Illithids raised in untended pools, without an elder brain to cull and tend them. They age oddly and come out wrong in individual ways: asymmetric, oversized or undersized, missing a faculty and compensating with another. Unthinned was Glyth's word for a tadpole that should have been culled. They took it.

They are the bulk of the colony's illithids, they are loyal because of what N'ghathrod did for them, and they are entirely themselves. Only three of them have ever been taken, and all three are among the enna.

As a template: apply to a mind flayer or arcanist. Remove something visible on the body — no Extract Brain, or no Mind Blast, or telepathy out to only 30 ft. Add +2 AC, +30 HP, and one malformation feature: 10 ft. tentacle reach, a second Mind Blast per rest, immunity to blindness, or a 10 ft. psychic aura. All of them have advantage on saves against being charmed or frightened; nothing cultivated them, so nothing reaches them easily.

N'ghathrod Act II antagonist

Founder of the colony. A mind flayer roughly a century into his second life, and a spacefaring elf before that — ceremorphosis destroyed the elf, and the Rod piece returned part of him. He is the only illithid alive who is two things at once, and he experiences it as having been healed.

He is also the ship. Fused into Vth'oramu for forty or fifty years, so every n'ghathu aboard was grown from his own flesh. Most of the colony lives inside him and thinks the ship is a ship.

He believes the voice directing him is his severed elder brain grown into something greater. It is Vecna.

Vocath's Operation

A mercane trade operation running a private grudge campaign against Mirt. A trading house with a grievance, run by a merchant who believes his quarrel is the whole of it. Full account in the Remaking.

The eye-in-a-wheel Vocath's own merchant mark. It appears on his goods wherever they move, which is why it turns up on the recovered rod, on the Sky Dock crate, and in the Bellwether Crow's last transmission from the Cairn approach. It marks a merchant's cargo.

It is a private house mark, so the Fleet registry has no entry for it. FR-016's cross-reference returned nothing, and WM-002 records it as matching nothing in registry. Anyone who trades in the right circles reads it on sight. Spectrum

Mid-tier ideological and operational factions across the cosmos.

Overlap with the Seekers These are independent forces with their own histories. Some of their cells are Seeker chapters that grew in that direction — a Fracture Cult cell that was taught how to ask, an Archivist cell whose founding story is the wrong shape. Most are exactly what they appear to be. The overlap is the camouflage, and it is why no institution has ever assembled the picture.

Concordance

An ideological force concerned with legitimacy, coherence, and metaphysical orthodoxy. Members work to define which version of reality is acceptable and to enforce that definition. Appear in the world as scholars, adjudicators, ritual authorities, doctrinal reformers, and institutional purists.

Fracture Cult

Exists to create peak events. Members hold that rupture, catastrophe, concentration of will, and symbolic climax can break stagnant reality open and reveal higher truth, power, or destiny. Accelerate worlds toward Zenith conditions through engineered crises and symbolic acts intended to force history into a sharper shape.

Archivists

Operate on monoliths. Drawn to monolith sites, incomplete archives, corrupted records, and the preservation or redirection of resonant meaning. Depending on the cell, members approach monoliths as sacred texts, exploitable instruments, historical treasures, or mechanisms to be controlled before others can reach them.

Syndicate

A durable criminal-commercial network that profits wherever order weakens. Trades in information, transport, salvage, black-market relics, smuggling, mercenary arrangements, and access. Operates with no particular ideology — instability pays.

Restorers Locked

An order spanning worlds, working to recover what the cosmos has lost. They keep archives of vanished civilizations, survey dead worlds, study Monolith lore, and trace pathways through the Continuum toward the Core — which their doctrine names the Mother of the Universe. They hold that reaching it would let them put back what catastrophe, archival failure and metaphysical erasure have taken.

Restorers is Fleet cataloguing, from a Watch intelligence summary four centuries old that has been copied forward ever since. The order's own term for what it does is the Return, and a member is someone who keeps the Return. They answer to the outside name when it is used, because arguing about it would cost more than it is worth.

Temperament Scholars, archivists, grave-surveyors and field historians. Patient, courteous, generous with what they know in exchange for what they want, and entirely sincere. They have no armies and no world of their own. The danger they represent is the thing they are competent enough to achieve.

The oldest chapter Locked

The Restorers are a Seeker chapter. They are the first one, and the only one that outgrew the world it began on.

Their founding account is the Seeker founding account, and they hold it as history rather than legend. A scholar on a world the order no longer names was working through the loss of a library — a real library, with a real fire, on a world with a real crater where a city used to be. Something spoke to her once. It gave her a way to establish that a thing had been lost, and a way to tell the difference between a thing lost and a thing that never was. It said nothing about recovery. She spent the rest of her life applying the method, and the order grew out of the people she taught.

That world is gone. The order survived it, which is the event that turned a chapter into an institution: a body of scholars whose own home became an entry in their own ledger. Everything that is unusual about the Restorers follows from that century. They crossed to other worlds because they had to. They learned to operate without a territory. They stopped expecting to be protected by anyone.

They read the resemblance backwards Restorer field scholars have met Seeker chapters, catalogued their practices, and noted the structural likeness. Their conclusion is that these are devotional imitations of the Return — fragments of the order's own method, carried outward by travellers and degraded into local faiths. The order considers itself the source. It is the eldest sibling, and it believes it is the parent.

Their mark is Seeker technique: the subject rendered as the absence. A horizon, a sky, and the curve of a world's edge, with the world itself left as bare material — cut through stone, unlined on vellum, an open gap in a woven border. A party that has seen one chapter's mark elsewhere will recognize the method before they recognize the order.

Doctrine & vocabulary

The Return rests on four claims, taught in this order and tested in this order.

  1. Loss is real and it is countable. A world, a people, a language, a discipline or a history either existed or did not, and the difference can be established by evidence. Grief is a separate matter and is not the order's business.
  2. Nothing lost is destroyed. The cosmos keeps a record of itself. Sites hold what happened to them; residue holds what it came from; the Core holds everything.
  3. The record can be read, and what is read can be restored. This is the claim the order has never tested, because the Core has never been reached.
  4. Restoration is owed. Not optional, not merciful — owed, by whoever is capable of it, to whoever was taken. A Restorer who could restore a world and declines has committed the order's only real sin.

Their working vocabulary, which is how the crew will identify one in conversation before they identify the order:

The LedgerThe accumulated record of established losses. Distributed across cells in deliberately partial copies, so that any one holding is incomplete and no one holds all of it.
A readingThe act of establishing a loss to the order's standard. Also the measure of a member's standing: a Restorer is spoken of by how many readings they have carried, from a First through a Fifth.

Structure & readings

Cells of four to nine, each with a keeper, each holding one segment of the Ledger and one assigned region. A cell knows its two neighbouring cells and the courier who serves all three. This is inherited Seeker practice, kept because it has twice been the reason the order survived a hostile government.

Standing is measured in readings rather than offices, and the ladder is a real ladder — each step is a thing the member has done, witnessed and filed.

The Fifth Readings are the order's whole exposure. Everything below them is archival work that any reasonable institution could tolerate. They are the ones pursuing a route to the Core, and they are four people with no protection, no state behind them, and a research programme that two separate cosmic powers would very much like to read.

What they actually do

Day to day, the order is a survey operation with a metaphysical thesis attached. Their field work is the most useful thing in the cosmos to a crew that needs to know what happened somewhere.

  • Dead-world survey. Landing on worlds that ended, and establishing what they were. They are the best source in the setting on any ruin, and they will trade.
  • Archive recovery. Buying, copying, and occasionally stealing records from institutions that are letting them rot. They regard a neglected archive as a loss in progress.
  • Monolith lore. Catalogued Monoliths for centuries, because doctrine holds the Core to be one of them. Their records on Monolith siting are better than the Order's, and the Fonains entry — a Monolith that stood and then did not — is the single most studied item in the Ledger.
  • Residue work. They recognize Reach residue as material from a loss, and they want it studied rather than used. An instrument in a Restorer's hands gets measured and written up. It does not get fired.
  • The approach. The Fifth Readings' work: pathways, thresholds, and the question of how a living person crosses into the Core and returns.

They do not coordinate with the Watchers, and most Watchers are unaware they exist. The upper echelon of the Order and senior Bridge officers in the Fleet hold fragments — the catalogue name, a handful of known contacts, and a standing judgement that the order is harmless and odd.

The ones who went in Locked

If everything lost is held in the archive, then the archive is where a Restorer needs to be. So they follow Zeniths, and let themselves be taken.

It works. The taking is bodily, and what it takes arrives at the Citadel alive. Across four centuries the order has put a great many of its best people into dying worlds on purpose, and a great many of them are in there right now.

Success and failure look identical from outside There is no return channel. A Restorer who walks into a Zenith is simply gone, whether the Monolith took them or the world killed them first. The order cannot tell which. That section of the Ledger is filed as failures, and it is more accurately an inventory of people they believe may have arrived.

It is the practice outsiders find hardest to credit, and it is why the Order calls them a death cult. From inside it is arithmetic: the thing they want is on the other side of a door that opens one way, so someone has to go through and someone has to keep going through until one of them finds a way to send word back.

Nobody ever has. That is the quiet fact underneath four centuries of volunteers, and the Fifth Readings have begun to argue about what it means.

What restoration would actually take DM only
Right about the method Their reasoning holds at every step that can be checked. Loss is real and countable. The cosmos keeps a record. Monoliths take bodily, and what they take goes to the Core. The Core can be reached, because mortals can make a crossing gods cannot. The Fifth Readings are neither deluded nor fools, and if the approach is solvable these are the people who will solve it.

The claim they have never tested is the last one: that what is read can be restored. Four centuries of work rests on it and no part of it has ever been demonstrated, because nothing has ever come back out of a Monolith.

Their whole evidence is one unrepeatable event The Citadel exists. A population went into the Core and their descendants are living in there, which from outside looks exactly like proof that the Core gives things back. It gave nothing back. It took the people of the First World while standing directly over them, and that has never recurred, because every Monolith since has stood somewhere else and every Zenith since has been a smaller thing. The order is reading the only evidence there is, and the evidence cannot repeat.

What restoration would actually require is three things, and the order has one of them. The Monolith that archived the world, which they can find. The world's heart as the catalyst — which persists after its world is destroyed, so a dead world still has a door standing in empty space. And the capacity to shape reality, which means all four fragments, seventeen pieces, enough axiomity to bend a thing back into being.

Nobody holds that. Vecna has suppositions. A Restorer inside the Citadel has had centuries with nothing to do but reason it out, and may be the only being in the cosmos who has assembled the whole requirement — sealed in the archive, unable to tell the order outside that its programme is two thirds short.

And it is achievable This is a real win, not a cruel joke. Worlds can be restored, the order is pointed in a defensible direction, and the thing standing between them and it is scale rather than error. What they would get is also smaller than what they imagine — a heart is a doorway and a catalyst rather than a preserved civilization, so restoration begins with a place to walk into and something to carry out, and the rebuilding is work rather than a miracle.
Why they are worth keeping They are honest, they keep their bargains, they survey worlds nobody else is counting, and they have put more of their own people into Zeniths than any institution has lost to one. They are also the only road to the Core, which is where the archive is, where the sect that wants out is, and where the one being who understands the antagonist has been sitting for an age. A crew can work beside a cell for a long time, owe them, and like them.

At the table
  • They arrive as help. A cell is researching the same ruin, the same disappearance, the same ship that stopped reporting. They share first and ask second, and their ask is a question rather than a favour.
  • They are the cosmos's reference desk. Any time the crew needs to know what a dead place used to be, a Restorer cell is the answer, and the price is a reading the crew can supply from where they have been.
  • Standing losses put them in the party's path. A world being emptied now outranks the Ledger, which means the Vth'orama and the Restorers are already working the same ground from different ends. The order has a file on the eye-in-a-wheel mark and no idea whose it is.
  • The reveal arrives as a sequence rather than a scene. Recognize the mark's technique. Then recognize the same technique on a chapter the crew already met. Then read the providence entries in order. Then find the first entry.
  • A Fifth Reading is a high-value target and knows it. Four people hold the approach work. The colony would take one. Vecna would rather they finished first.

Runekeepers Working name

A small, hidden order on an unfindable world. Practitioners of runic transit — a discipline of marking celestial bodies in ways that attune the practitioner to that body, and through it, to the body's heart. Their existence in the wider cosmos is rumor at best.

Their homeworld defends itself by being unfindable. A ritual held by the order's senior practitioner, inscribed on that practitioner's own body, withdraws the world from cosmological visibility; the working draws on the oscillation of the Dissolution fragment the order keeps, so the concealment rides on a cycle and the ritual is what points it. The world sits where it has always sat, reachable by anyone who knows the way back. An exiled practitioner sent into the wider cosmos is given no map and no name; the order's custom is that home must be re-recognized through remembered sensory and cultural fragments.

Other arcane disciplines do not recognize what Runekeepers do as the same kind of work as Weave-based transit. The bypass of Forbiddance follows from this: the order's mode of travel is not Weave-based, and Forbiddance is specified against Weave-based teleportation. The deeper diagnostic is that a Wayfinder can only attune to bodies that have a heart in the cosmologically meaningful sense — a constructed installation does not qualify.

Wayfinders

Members of the order sent out into the wider cosmos for assigned purposes — to learn, to recover, to witness, or to bring something back. A Wayfinder carries a partial mark — the inscription their training has earned them — which grows as their understanding does. A Wayfinder with a complete mark can attune to a celestial body via ritual and time, opening a transit route between attuned points.

Astra Virex
Wounded celestial
A conscious body that fell. Wounded, calling people to it, and expanding through those who answer.

A sentient paracausal celestial body, wounded by a Seam — Transformation and Boundary overlapping across something that was already conscious, opening its distinction and altering its nature at once. Astra is what the Order calls it from the outside; Virex is nearer to what it is. Not a god in the doctrinal sense; functions like one in the experience of those tethered to it.

What it wants: to be whole, to be strong, and to never let this happen to it again. It is neither good nor evil. It is hurt, and it is acting on that.

Its method is calling. It reaches across distance, finds someone who can hear it, and draws them toward it. Those who answer and are changed by it are Starchildren — practitioners whose existence extends its presence into the regions they inhabit, altering the cosmological character of those regions over time.

What it does not know Astra Virex is oriented toward constellations. It has no knowledge of world-hearts. It may only learn of them through a Starchild who touches one — which is a turn, not a given.

Open: how much of what it was survived the Seam, and whether anything of the original is recoverable.
