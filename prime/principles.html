Principles

How this table is run. First the DM Toolkit, which holds at any table: the foundations, the four pillars (exploration, social, combat, running the game), the pre-session gap check and a self-assessment. Then the Caelestis rules: prep, the shape of a story, canon and scope, hooks, the players, NPCs, set pieces and contracts. These are the working rules for this table. The campaign's own shape and arcs are on Campaign; what happened at each session is on Voyages; how a page of this wiki is built is in the repository's page guide, prime/docs/PAGE-GUIDE.md.

Foundations

The DM Toolkit is how this DM runs any table, whatever the campaign: two foundations, then the four pillars (Exploration, Social, Combat, and Running the Game). Each pillar has a principle, read before prep and again in a session review, and an apparatus, the template or checklist that makes the principle happen under pressure at the table. The Caelestis rules follow from Prep onward.

Prep situations, not scripts

A prepped scene is a situation: a setting (place, time, atmosphere), NPCs who each want something, pressures already in motion before the crew arrives, and the directions it could move depending on what the crew does. The scene moves because the people in it act on their wants and the pressures answer the crew. The usual failure is a plot built for the players to walk through: beats to hit, lines to say, a room to reach.

The test: if the prep says what the players do, it is a script. If it says what is true and what everyone wants, it is a situation. How this campaign applies it is under Situations, not scripts.

Motivation is the generative unit

Every NPC who matters has a concrete want before the session. When a player asks something unprepped, the answer is read off that want; without it, the DM stitches motivation on the fly and it shows. "Wants power" is useless. A want has to be specific enough to produce behaviour. This is the weakest link and the one that pays back most.

Exploration

This table explores a place it has been pointed at, with a purpose: it needs something and goes to get it, breaking rules if it has to. Heists, infiltration, going through the mayor's desk. Build for that style.

  • Make the pointed-at place worth exploring. A place with its own agenda and pushback rewards poking at it. A place that sits still while the players pursue a goal is a set.
  • Keep connective tissue cheap. Travel, rests and the random village get low-prep reusable tools such as random tables. The real prep goes to the places that matter.
  • Secrets are tangible and gettable. Every notable place can hide a secret, and every key NPC has one the crew can go and get: a letter in a drawer, a ledger in a safe, a scar under a sleeve, a name carved where no one looks. The secret is the reward at the end of the exploration and usually feeds a social scene afterward. The move to make happen: the crew loses or skips a social route, decides to get the information another way, and breaks some rules to do it.

Making secrets tangible and giving this table exploration it will actually do are the same fix. A face-value world has nothing to dig for.

Site template

For every location that is more than a corridor.

  • What it wants, what it is doing. Its own agenda or process. If the crew did nothing, the place would still be doing something; if it would not, give it an agenda.
  • How it pushes back. What the place does when the crew acts on it: it tightens, shifts, notices, responds.
  • What changes as they act. The state that evolves. Room 3 is different because of what they did in room 1.
  • Cost of presence. What it takes to be here and whether it climbs: time, resources, danger, attention. It makes staying a decision.
  • The secret. What is hidden, where, and how it can be got: search, stealth, a social pretext, a key found elsewhere. Every key NPC tied to the place has a secret reachable here.
  • Inhabitants. Anyone who lives or works here gets their own want. The place often pushes back through them.

Social

A social encounter is a contest of wants that resolves into a changed state. The NPC wants something from the exchange and is never there only to hand out information. The players want something: information, a favour, access, an escape, cover for a lie, a read on a liar. Tension comes from what is at risk, and the scene ends with something different: the information got or lost, a favour earned or a bridge burned, a lie sold or suspicion raised, an ally or an enemy made.

Social encounter template
  • NPC wants. What they are after in this exchange, in one sentence.
  • Players' want. What the crew came to get. If it is unclear, the scene may not need to exist.
  • Source of tension. Wants in conflict, a cost to asking, a guarded secret, suspicion, a relationship on the line, a lie that could unravel.
  • What success and failure change. The state the scene leaves behind.

The test, shared with combat: what does the other side want, and what does winning or losing change? An encounter that cannot answer both is a set piece to execute.

Combat

A fight is a contest of wants too. The players want something beyond survival. The enemy wants something and will do more than trade blows to get it: retreat, negotiate, take a hostage, finish a task, flee with the prize. The fight ends at its dramatic peak, and an end other than every enemy at 0 HP is the norm. Pure attrition grinds, runs long and ends when the maths runs out.

A clock gives a fight urgency and shape: a consequence that lands if it drags, such as a ritual completing, a breach spreading, reinforcements arriving, a hostage's time running out. "Kill them" becomes "kill them before it happens". Use it when the encounter wants urgency; it is not required. Long sessions come from turn-level friction and redundant fights more than from enemy count.

Combat build

Does the fight earn its place?

  • An objective beyond zeroing hit points: hold the door, reach the console, protect the NPC, escape, deny the enemy its goal.
  • What the enemy wants, and what it will do besides trade blows.
  • A clock, if the fight wants urgency.
  • An end condition other than a total kill: morale breaks, the objective is met, a retreat, the clock runs out.

Will it run tight?

  • Enemy count fits the table. Fewer, tougher bodies beat swarms; every extra creature is bookkeeping every round.
  • Reanimation, respawns and refreshing summons are capped or cut, so they never reset the party's progress.
  • Two or three systems tracked at once at most (adds, boss, environment, effects).
  • A boss carries legendary or lair actions, so its turn has weight without more creatures on the board.
  • No fight repeats the one before or after it. Two fights may be better as one.
  • Prompt the on-deck player so they decide during the turn before. This is the single biggest drain.

Running the game
  • Never hard-block a direction for lack of prep. A thread the players pull becomes real. Bend it toward the spine; the wants of the NPCs and places travel into unprepped ground and keep the improvising consistent.
  • Every choice returns something. Information, a complication, a lead, a cost, a reward; maybe not at once, but something. Before planting a hook, know what is at the end of it, or leave it unplanted.
  • Failure closes a door, never the campaign. A failed roll or a lost scene shuts that route and pushes the crew to find another, now harder. The exception is the genuine dice-decide-it moment, such as the last PC standing in the final battle; that exception is what gives every recoverable failure its weight.
  • Rewards are earned, and broad. Loot is earned by engaging and clearing a challenge, given neither freely nor stingily, and fits the challenge. A reward can be a consumable, a favour owed, information, access, a contact, inspiration or a secret uncovered, as well as an item.
  • Goals spread, spotlight rotates. The spine hangs on two or three players' direct motivations at a time. The rest have a real reason to be there, and support ("our friends need this") is a real reason. Whose arc leads shifts over the campaign.
  • Nudge logistics, never decisions. Players choose where the story happens; the DM makes sure it happens somewhere runnable. The tools: a recap that frames their choices as consequences; a wandering conduit, an NPC or force who can plausibly lead them to playable ground; and the reasonable assumption ("you regroup and head to X") when they have cornered themselves.
  • Backstory hooks into the campaign. Every PC has a thread to the spine that surfaces when the story is ready. A backstory thread is a want and a pressure to pull on, never a plot the player must walk through.

Pre-session gap check

Five minutes before calling a scene prepped.

  1. Every NPC in the scene has a concrete want (Foundations).
  2. The location wants or does something of its own, and hides a tangible, gettable secret (Exploration).
  3. If the players go sideways, the prep extends; it leans on no script (Foundations, Running the game).
  4. Every hook planted has a known payoff (Running the game).
  5. Every load-bearing clue is reachable three ways (below).
  6. Every fight, conversation and dig has a want on both sides and a resolution that changes something: the combat build on every fight, the social template on every weighted scene, a secret in every notable place (Exploration, Social, Combat).
  7. If they fail a roll or lose a scene, the second route is known (Running the game).
  8. A fair nudge is ready if they corner themselves, touching logistics only (Running the game).

Clue redundancy, the rule of three: a clue the spine depends on is reachable through a person who can tell it, a place or object where it can be found, and a third path such as a second NPC, something overheard or an inference. The crew still has to pull the thread. Flavour clues need only one path.

Self-assessment

Where the DM stands, so the toolkit knows what it is correcting for. Update it as it changes.

  • Strengths. Strong creative and improvisational instincts: most sessions land, and players have fun through hard pivots. The agency-preserving nudge already happens; naming it makes it deliberate. Intricate backstory hooks are a strength to keep feeding. Absent players stay alive in the fiction.
  • NPC foundations. The weakest link: plot to walk through instead of situations with wants. Fix: a want for every NPC before the session.
  • Exploration and secrets. Worlds played at face value, with secrets kept in NPCs' heads where nobody can trigger them. Fix: physical, gettable secrets and purpose-driven digging. The highest-leverage change for this table.
  • Encounters without stakes. Fights and talks with no want on the other side and no changed state; in combat this also runs sessions long. Fix: every encounter a contest of wants that resolves.
  • Loot. Rewarding well is hard. Fix: earned, and broad.
  • Clueing. More generous with hints without handing them over. Fix: generosity through reachability, the rule of three.

The through-line: imagination is ahead of finishing discipline. The toolkit brings the finishing up to the imagination by hardening the two or three seams where the prep reliably drifts.

Prep
  • A named world has core facts. A world that is named has its core facts even if nothing else is built: what it is like and who lives there. Anything named in play needs work from that point on.
  • Prep one ring out. Prep where the crew is going and one ring out from it: H'Catha and the cantina now. Seekers chapters and other far details are built when they become active in the larger shape, and left alone until then.
  • Progress before worlds. The opposition's progress is tracked as one measure the DM sets as the story needs, and the marks and worlds behind it are built when play reaches them.

Story shape

Players bend tempo, not direction

The larger plot continues whatever the players do, and how fast and how cleanly it advances depends on their choices. Every major outcome resolves into several states, and all of them feed forward. The Fork at graduation is the model. The players can make the opposition work harder, take losses, change tactics and miss windows; holding a key item never hard-stops the plot.

Situations, not scripts
  • Put situations into the world. Let the characters respond, or do nothing. A thread is never scripted toward an outcome.
  • A mission is a nudge. The crew arrives mid-chapter into a situation already running, and it continues if they leave.
  • Story is portable. A few key worlds carry fixed story. Floating situations move to wherever the crew goes.
  • Scenes have walls. Railroading is acceptable only inside a situation the crew chose, and only until it ends.
  • One thing, then another. The party goes in thinking it is one thing, and it turns out to be another, without leaving the path.
  • The goal is always known. The party always knows its goal. How they reach it is theirs, and the DM supplies the tools.

Villain planning at three resolutions

An antagonist's plan runs at three resolutions: a fixed end, fixed major steps, and one detailed next step. When the crew interferes, the next step changes and the end stays: A is the plan, B happened, C is the response, and they still have to reach D.

The clock starts with the crew

The eye's moment has no date until the crew takes up the inscription. When they begin to work against it, the DM sets the date, and the timeline counts from there. Until then the clock stands still, and the campaign never fails behind the players' backs for something they missed. The working is on Opposition: Vecna.

Hooks are directions

A hook's "if pulled" is a direction, never a script. If the players pull it, that direction becomes real and develops. If they pull it somewhere unexpected, bend the destination and keep a route back to the larger shape. Unpulled hooks stay planted for later, so a hook is never wasted.

Asking for a roll means something is there

When the players are interested in something and the DM calls for a roll, there is something to find, whatever the result. The roll sets how much they get and how fast. A low roll shows that something is there: two people talking in hushed tones, words out of earshot. A high roll gives more at once: twenty gold changing hands, and a look around to check no one saw.

Context still matters. A check for a specific thing can simply fail; a search for a secret door may find no door. A check for whether something feels off always finds that something does.

When nothing is there, call for no roll. A roll means there is a chance for something to happen. If a path will reward neither the players' time nor the DM's, do not entertain it.

Put needed things in the crew's way. When something matters for the story, set it where they will meet it instead of hoping they look. Lean less on "what do you want to do?": offer the nudge that something is here, and that there is something in it for them if they go for it.

Encounter tables

Every entry on an encounter table points to a logged encounter that can be run as written. A one-line prompt is a note, never an entry.

The DM has the same tools as the players

This is still Dungeons & Dragons in a sci-fi skin. Anything a player can do, the world can do: spells, charms, mass suggestion, mind blank, a familiar on watch. When a plan in the world needs people silent, loyal or unaware, reach for the spell that would do it before inventing a reason they just did not notice. Using these is not cheating. Let the spectacle show, too: a place held by magic should feel uncanny, not merely quiet.

Reskin first

If an existing item, feature or subclass does the job, reskin it, and keep custom things to a minimum. An NPC ally is a light version of a real subclass with that subclass's own resource: Saerthe as a Materializer, Tarto as a Battle Master.

Feature and mechanic text uses D&D Beyond 2024 phrasing and structure: "Choose a creature you can see", not "When you can see a creature".

Canon and scope

Fonains came first

Caelestis's cosmology conforms to Fonains. Lola's Boys came first, and everything here builds off what happened there. When a ruling here would contradict Fonains canon, Fonains wins.

Between the published worlds and the homebrew

Caelestis runs the published Spelljammer Academy through Realmspace, the published Forgotten Realms. The campaign lives between those published worlds and a homebrew cosmology. Treat the published material as backdrop and junction, and surface canon references as breadcrumbs (Toril, Mirt, Saerthe, Bral) rather than the main story.

  • Name canon accurately. When a published thing appears, name it correctly.
  • Leave canon alone. The campaign is never about settling published lore.
  • Use the contrast. The familiar published world is what makes the homebrew weight felt.
  • New worlds from published settings. Where possible, new worlds come from published settings: Azeroth as a possible one-shot, Exandria without its Critical Role events, Drakkenheim.

Most trouble is local

Some factions are Vecna's and many are not. Some trouble is a Zenith and much of it is local politics. A contract surfaces because of the state of the world, and the place around it should feel like it exists without the crew.

One strain, many expressions

The Axiom strain shows at every scale. When the wider cosmos strains, so do single worlds, factions, NPCs and the crew. A strange event on a world is rarely only that world's problem: it is usually a local expression of the strain, and the same strain will surface in the crew's arcs too. Paracausality is its most common local expression.

New material wants to belong

The campaign's theme is that everyone is missing something and is trying to become whole (Campaign). New material should want to belong somewhere: a new NPC, faction or world arrives already reaching for something it lacks.

Iconic items

Famous D&D items can exist in this cosmos, and recognised power is suspicious here. The things that matter to the larger story mostly carry no famous name. Bring an iconic item in when it says something about a being, a past project or what a faith values; leave it out when it would be a cameo. The test is whether the item fits this cosmos's logic, not D&D's.

Physical movement matters

Fleet installations of consequence are sealed against teleportation, and Caelestis is one (its security). An operation against a hardened target arrives as someone and leaves on a ship, which is why months of reconnaissance carry weight. Gods cannot stand on worlds because of polarity, so Vecna works through vessels; the two rules are separate and point the same way.

Hooks

Five kinds of hook land at the table, and each is pulled a different way. The kinds are the type tags on Plot Hooks.

Character
Met through a person: an NPC who knows something, wants something, or is caught up in something. The NPC is the door.
Place
Met by going somewhere. The place holds the hook, and arriving triggers it.
Event
Met by an incident: an attack, an arrival, a discovery, a death. The crew is present, and the event opens the thread.
Object
Met through a thing in hand: an item that hums, a record that says too much, a sigil nobody can identify.
Pattern
Met by noticing connections. Several small incidents together imply something larger, and only the crew can recognise it.

Most hooks combine kinds. The H'Catha meteor is an object and an event; the Latchling is a place, an object and a character. Tagging a hook with every kind that fits lets it surface from several angles.

The players
  • Push and pull. Sol's and Bartholomew's players want direct hooks and a push. Casey's and Tumak's pull on mystery unprompted. Gregory's sits between.
  • Reveal a mechanic by player. Set up a situation where it fires for the discovery players (Tumak), give it up front to Bartholomew, keep Casey's vague, and give Sol, Gregory and Boogie concrete material.
  • Mechanic in full, meaning held back. Give a player a mechanic in full and hold back what it means.
  • A thread each. Each PC keeps a distinct, bespoke thread. Beats are things players interact with, never long narration or "you have a dream".
  • Separate scenes. Two PCs with business in the same room means one of them loses, so place arc beats in separate scenes.

NPCs and connections
  • Every NPC brings a hook. Each new NPC arrives with a hook of their own. Pffred and Krik'Lit came aboard as just another person each, and the table felt it.
  • Twice, or say it. Subtlety only goes so far. Put a connection in front of the players twice, or have an NPC remark on it.
  • NPCs hang back. With a crowded deck, NPCs engage only when a player engages them, or when one has to act as the person they are.
  • Visions sparingly. Use visions sparingly; there have been many. Polly's voice nudges Casey toward things and never directs him.

Set pieces and crawls

Set pieces
  • Keep the mechanics simple. One rule per encounter, shown once. The V006 Command Deck fight was a slog for an online table.
  • NPCs leave the puzzles alone. An NPC never solves a puzzle for the players.
  • Show everything on the board. Any fight on the board shows every source as a token, with fog off. Roll minions in clusters.
  • Threshold cards. Read a three-line card per room before any token drops: first sense, dominant feature, one thing that moves. Strip borrowed map props or declare them set dressing, and note height on the token.

Table practice for crawls
  • Theatre of mind aboard ship. Ship exploration runs theatre of mind, with detailed notes per level and no map.
  • Push non-combat abilities. Name non-combat uses of abilities out loud.
  • Say yes, charge a resource. Say yes to the clever thing and charge a resource for it, rather than a roll.
  • Keep the ship talking. Keep ship chatter going so the ship never feels empty.
  • State the goal, then supply obstacles. In V005 the crew engineered the whole traversal from one stated goal.

Handing an ally's block to the players

An ally's stat block is worth publishing once the crew has earned it. Knowing that Tarto can spend her reaction to swap places with a cadet, or that anyone who casts mending can repair Saerthe's cubes, turns an NPC from a passenger into someone the party can plan around. The block is written once on the DM dossier; publishing it means adding the same tab to that character's public page. Start with whichever NPC the crew has bonded with, and treat it as a reward, not a reference release.
