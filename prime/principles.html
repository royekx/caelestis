Principles

How this campaign is run: prep, the shape of a story, canon and scope, hooks, the players, NPCs, set pieces and contracts. These are the working rules learned at this table so far. The campaign's own shape and arcs are on Campaign; what happened at each session is on Voyages; how a page of this wiki is built is in the repository's page guide, docs/PAGE-GUIDE.md.

Prep
  • A named world has core facts. A world that is named has its core facts even if nothing else is built: what it is like and who lives there. Anything named in play needs work from that point on.
  • Prep one ring out. Prep where the crew is going and one ring out from it: H'Catha and the cantina now. Seekers chapters and other far details are built when they become active in the larger shape, and left alone until then.
  • Counts before worlds. The opposition's progress is tracked as counts, hearts found, opened and linked, and the worlds behind the counts are built when play reaches them.

Story shape

Players bend tempo, not direction

The larger plot continues whatever the players do, and how fast and how cleanly it advances depends on their choices. Every major outcome resolves into several states, and all of them feed forward. The Fork at graduation is the model. The players can make the opposition work harder, take losses, change tactics and miss windows; holding a key item never hard-stops the plot.

Situations, not scripts
  • Put situations into the world. Let the characters respond, or do nothing. A thread is never scripted toward an outcome.
  • A mission is a nudge. The crew arrives mid-chapter into a situation already running, and it continues if they leave.
  • Story is portable. A few key worlds carry fixed story. Floating situations move to wherever the crew goes.
  • Scenes have walls. Railroading is acceptable only inside a situation the crew chose, and only until it ends.
  • One thing, then another. The party goes in thinking it is one thing, and it turns out to be another, without leaving the path.
  • The goal is always known. The party always knows its goal. How they reach it is theirs, and the DM supplies the tools.

Villain planning at three resolutions

An antagonist's plan runs at three resolutions: a fixed end, fixed major steps, and one detailed next step. When the crew interferes, the next step changes and the end stays: A is the plan, B happened, C is the response, and they still have to reach D.

Hooks are directions

A hook's "if pulled" is a direction, never a script. If the players pull it, that direction becomes real and develops. If they pull it somewhere unexpected, bend the destination and keep a route back to the larger shape. Unpulled hooks stay planted for later, so a hook is never wasted.

Canon and scope

Fonains came first

Caelestis's cosmology conforms to Fonains. Lola's Boys came first, and everything here builds off what happened there. When a ruling here would contradict Fonains canon, Fonains wins.

Between the published worlds and the homebrew

Caelestis runs the published Spelljammer Academy through Realmspace, the published Forgotten Realms. The campaign lives between those published worlds and a homebrew cosmology. Treat the published material as backdrop and junction, and surface canon references as breadcrumbs (Toril, Mirt, Saerthe, Bral) rather than the main story.

  • Name canon accurately. When a published thing appears, name it correctly.
  • Leave canon alone. The campaign is never about settling published lore.
  • Use the contrast. The familiar published world is what makes the homebrew weight felt.
  • New worlds from published settings. Where possible, new worlds come from published settings: Azeroth as a possible one-shot, Exandria without its Critical Role events, Drakkenheim.

Most trouble is local

Some factions are Vecna's and many are not. Some trouble is a Zenith and much of it is local politics. A contract surfaces because of the state of the world, and the place around it should feel like it exists without the crew.

One strain, many expressions

The Axiom strain shows at every scale. When the wider cosmos strains, so do single worlds, factions, NPCs and the crew. A strange event on a world is rarely only that world's problem: it is usually a local expression of the strain, and the same strain will surface in the crew's arcs too. Paracausality is its most common local expression.

New material wants to belong

The campaign's theme is that everyone is missing something and is trying to become whole (Campaign). New material should want to belong somewhere: a new NPC, faction or world arrives already reaching for something it lacks.

Iconic items

Famous D&D items can exist in this cosmos, and recognised power is suspicious here. The things that matter to the larger story mostly carry no famous name. Bring an iconic item in when it says something about a being, a past project or what a faith values; leave it out when it would be a cameo. The test is whether the item fits this cosmos's logic, not D&D's.

Physical movement matters

Fleet installations of consequence are sealed against teleportation, and Caelestis is one (its security). An operation against a hardened target arrives as someone and leaves on a ship, which is why months of reconnaissance carry weight. Gods cannot stand on worlds because of polarity, so Vecna works through vessels; the two rules are separate and point the same way.

Hooks

Five kinds of hook land at the table, and each is pulled a different way. The kinds are the type tags on Plot Hooks.

Character
Met through a person: an NPC who knows something, wants something, or is caught up in something. The NPC is the door.
Place
Met by going somewhere. The place holds the hook, and arriving triggers it.
Event
Met by an incident: an attack, an arrival, a discovery, a death. The crew is present, and the event opens the thread.
Object
Met through a thing in hand: an item that hums, a record that says too much, a sigil nobody can identify.
Pattern
Met by noticing connections. Several small incidents together imply something larger, and only the crew can recognise it.

Most hooks combine kinds. The H'Catha meteor is an object and an event; the Latchling is a place, an object and a character. Tagging a hook with every kind that fits lets it surface from several angles.

The players
  • Push and pull. Sol's and Bartholomew's players want direct hooks and a push. Casey's and Tumak's pull on mystery unprompted. Gregory's sits between.
  • Reveal a mechanic by player. Set up a situation where it fires for the discovery players (Tumak), give it up front to Bartholomew, keep Casey's vague, and give Sol, Gregory and Boogie concrete material.
  • Mechanic in full, meaning held back. Give a player a mechanic in full and hold back what it means.
  • A thread each. Each PC keeps a distinct, bespoke thread. Beats are things players interact with, never long narration or "you have a dream".
  • Separate scenes. Two PCs with business in the same room means one of them loses, so place arc beats in separate scenes.

NPCs and connections
  • Every NPC brings a hook. Each new NPC arrives with a hook of their own. Pffred and Krik'Lit came aboard as just another person each, and the table felt it.
  • Twice, or say it. Subtlety only goes so far. Put a connection in front of the players twice, or have an NPC remark on it.
  • NPCs hang back. With a crowded deck, NPCs engage only when a player engages them, or when one has to act as the person they are.
  • Visions sparingly. Use visions sparingly; there have been many. Polly's voice nudges Casey toward things and never directs him.

Set pieces and crawls

Set pieces
  • Keep the mechanics simple. One rule per encounter, shown once. The V006 Command Deck fight was a slog for an online table.
  • NPCs leave the puzzles alone. An NPC never solves a puzzle for the players.
  • Show everything on the board. Any fight on the board shows every source as a token, with fog off. Roll minions in clusters.
  • Threshold cards. Read a three-line card per room before any token drops: first sense, dominant feature, one thing that moves. Strip borrowed map props or declare them set dressing, and note height on the token.

Table practice for crawls
  • Theatre of mind aboard ship. Ship exploration runs theatre of mind, with detailed notes per level and no map.
  • Push non-combat abilities. Name non-combat uses of abilities out loud.
  • Say yes, charge a resource. Say yes to the clever thing and charge a resource for it, rather than a roll.
  • Keep the ship talking. Keep ship chatter going so the ship never feels empty.
  • State the goal, then supply obstacles. In V005 the crew engineered the whole traversal from one stated goal.

Handing an ally's block to the players

An ally's stat block is worth publishing once the crew has earned it. Knowing that Tarto can spend her reaction to swap places with a cadet, or that anyone who casts mending can repair Saerthe's cubes, turns an NPC from a passenger into someone the party can plan around. The block is written once on the DM dossier; publishing it means adding the same tab to that character's public page. Start with whichever NPC the crew has bonded with, and treat it as a reward, not a reference release.
