Perils of the Drift — Creatures and Hazards of the Crossing, Cadet Survival Edition Caelestis Corps · Cadet distribution

This manual covers the creatures and conditions most likely to threaten a crew on a crossing: life of the drift, boarders and their thralls, what waits in derelicts, the lesser kin of the beholders, and the hazards of the crossing itself. The peoples a crew meets, hostile ones included, are in Peoples of the Drift.

Each entry carries a threat grade. A Harmless creature will not hurt a crew that leaves it alone. A Hazard costs time, gear or health if it is handled badly, a Dangerous creature or condition can kill a crew member, and a Lethal one can kill a crew or its ship.

Entries use the Academy's fixed wording for harm. A creature that shrugs off half of a kind of harm takes half of it. A creature unharmed by a kind of harm takes none of it, and one that takes double from a kind of harm takes twice the usual. Where an entry names no such kind, the creature has none.

Drift life

Small drift life

Harmless
  • Signs. Space guppies are fish two to three feet long that glide through the drift; small creatures sometimes ride them. Space mollymawks are scavenging birds with ten-foot wings, as common in the drift as albatrosses are at sea, and they perch on ship hulls. Space swine are winged boars about five feet long, bred by dohwar as mounts and pack animals.
  • Behaviour. A mollymawk moves from one ship's air envelope to another's when it needs to and lingers wherever food is reliable, so a mollymawk on the rigging came from another envelope not far off. A guppy makes a small envelope of its own and can keep one tiny passenger alive in it.
  • Avoid or survive. Leave them be. Sailors hold that killing a mollymawk brings bad luck.

Giant space hamster

Harmless
  • Signs. A plant-eating rodent as big as a brown bear. It burrows.
  • Behaviour. Shy; it avoids other creatures, threatening ones above all. Trained, it serves as a mount and a beast of burden. Gnomes have tried to power spelljamming ships with giant space hamsters in wheels, so far without success.
  • Avoid or survive. A cornered hamster bites, so give it a way out.

Kindori

Harmless
  • Signs. A huge, gentle space whale with no visible mouth. Small eyes dot the leading edge of its body, and kindori talk to one another over long distances with flashes of light from them. A kindori is big enough to have its own gravity plane and air envelope, so smaller creatures live and travel on its back. Moss and mould grow on its hide, and scavvers may feed on them while it ignores them. Heavy overgrowth of vines and plants marks an old or sick kindori.
  • Behaviour. Peaceful. Kindori feed on the light of suns and stars and need no food, water or air. Solar and lunar dragons hunt them, and so do some of the peoples of the drift.
  • Strengths. A tail that strikes very hard, and eyes that can flash a cone of bright light about 120 feet long, blinding whoever in it fails to steel themselves, for up to a minute. A kindori flashes a predator and escapes while it is blind.
  • Weaknesses. A kindori is slow to fight and would rather flee.
  • Avoid or survive. Give it room, and keep weapons off it. Blindness from the flash wears off as the eyes recover, sooner for a sailor who keeps their head.

Space eels

Hazard
  • Signs. Eels about five feet long trailing a ship and feeding on barnacles they pry off its hull. Giant space eels travel in schools.
  • Behaviour. Space eels avoid larger creatures unless they are starving. When a school of giant space eels passes a ship, each eel throws a bolt of lightning as it goes, and the bolts start fires on deck. Cadets have flown through such a school in simulation.
  • Strengths. A space eel bites and latches on, and keeps chewing until it is pulled off. Its tail spine carries a venom that paralyses until the victim shakes it off. Eels need no air.
  • Weaknesses. A latched eel comes off to a firm pull, by its victim or by a crewmate.
  • Avoid or survive. Cross behind a school of giant eels, keep water and sand ready on deck, and assign hands to the fires before the school arrives. Wildspace hunters catch space eels for their meat, which is harder than it sounds.

Scavvers

Dangerous
  • Signs. Shark-like predators that follow larger creatures and ships to feed on what they leave. Gray scavvers are six feet long and hunt in packs. Brown scavvers run to ten feet and swallow prey whole, and night scavvers, fifteen feet long and patterned like the starry dark, come right into a ship's envelope and onto its deck. Void scavvers are twenty feet long, pitch-black and solitary.
  • Behaviour. Most scavvers live on scraps; a void scavver goes after the cook. Blood sends gray scavvers into a frenzy, though wounding one usually breaks its attack. A brown scavver digests a meal for days and fights only in self-defence while it does.
  • Strengths. A void scavver's bite tears deep and can swallow a creature up to the size of a horse whole. Inside, the swallowed sailor is blind, held fast and burned by acid moment by moment. Its eye throws an invisible ray of fear at one creature within about sixty feet. A brown scavver's stomach is full of poison gas that finishes whatever survives the swallowing. Scavvers need no air.
  • Weaknesses. A swallowed sailor who deals a void scavver a heavy wound from inside may force it to spit them out, and if it dies they can climb free of the body.
  • Avoid or survive. Keep galley waste off the side; a scavver following a ship has found its scraps. Keep crew in pairs on deck when a night scavver is about. Cooked night scavver is a popular tavern dish.

Astereater

Lethal
  • Signs. A beholder-kin as big as an asteroid, lying still at the outer edge of an asteroid field. While it stays still it is indistinguishable from the rocks around it.
  • Behaviour. It waits for a ship to come close, closes the gap fast, and bites and grapples the hull. It cannot digest metal or stone, so the metal and treasure of a ship it has eaten stay inside it intact.
  • Strengths. Size, strength and a perfect disguise. It sees a very long way in the dark.
  • Weaknesses. An astereater has little of the intelligence or magic of its kin.
  • Avoid or survive. Give the outer rocks of a field a wide berth, and post a watch for any rock that moves against the rest.

Star lancer

Dangerous
  • Signs. A large, blue, shark-like celestial with four wings and a horn on its head. The first sign is usually a distortion in space a moment before it appears.
  • Behaviour. It turns invisible, together with one rider of its choosing and everything both carry, closes unseen, and comes out of hiding at a charge. It speaks mind to mind out to about 120 feet.
  • Strengths. Its horn strikes hard, and much harder after a straight run of about twenty feet. It can fly out of an enemy's reach without giving the enemy a free strike, and it shrugs off half of radiant harm.
  • Weaknesses. Its invisibility ends the moment it or its rider attacks or casts a spell, and it holds the cloak by concentration, so a solid hit while it is hidden can break it. It can cloak itself only a few times a day.
  • Avoid or survive. Watch for the distortion, and keep weapons ready to strike the instant it shows itself. Radiant light is a poor choice against it.

Murder comet

Dangerous
  • Signs. A screaming stone head wreathed in flame, trailing fire across the drift and lighting the space around it. Murder comets travel alone or in packs.
  • Behaviour. Evil spellcasters make them by binding the essence of earth to the essence of fire. They hunt ships and crews to destroy them. A comet's maker can bind their own spirit into one and live on in it, ageless, and that comet wears its maker's face.
  • Strengths. Very fast. It slams and spits fire out to about sixty feet, and it does double damage to hulls and structures. It is unharmed by fire and poison, and it cannot be paralysed, petrified, poisoned, knocked prone, worn down or knocked out. It needs no air, food, drink or sleep.
  • Weaknesses. No kind of harm hurts it more than another. When it is destroyed it explodes, burning everyone within about twenty feet.
  • Avoid or survive. Bring it down at range and away from the hull, the sails and the crew.

Solar dragon

Lethal
  • Signs. A serpentine dragon with fin-tipped limbs, glowing nebulous wings and gleaming, many-coloured scales. Solar dragons are born in the hearts of stars and see clearly in the brightest light.
  • Behaviour. A territorial hunter of kindori, scavvers and other drift life. A young one may claim an asteroid belt, an old one a whole system. Solar dragons find humanoids violent and tiresome and usually avoid them. A solar dragon may mistake a distant ship for prey and come within range before it sees its error and veers off. An experienced captain does not take that approach as an attack; solar dragons that are fired on have battered ships to pieces.
  • Strengths. Its breath is a flashing mote of radiant energy that flies to a point up to about 180 feet away and bursts into a ball of light some sixty feet across. Its bite carries radiant harm, it can loose blinding bursts of light around itself, and it does double damage to hulls and structures. It is unharmed by radiant light, cannot be blinded, needs no air, and magic cannot read its mind. It throws off the first few spells that would bind it.
  • Weaknesses. No kind of harm hurts it more than another. Light and radiant spells are wasted on it.
  • Avoid or survive. Hold fire and let it veer away. If it fights, spread out so one burst catches few, and keep out of its lair.

Lunar dragon

Lethal
  • Signs. A dragon that lairs inside a desolate moon, burrowing through solid rock. Hatchlings are alabaster white and darken with age to slate.
  • Behaviour. Capricious and xenophobic. Lunar dragons enjoy keeping treasure from others more than owning it, and their hoards often hold spelljamming helms taken from ships that strayed into their territory.
  • Strengths. Its breath is a cone of frost about sixty feet long that freezes those it catches in place for a moment. Its bite carries cold. It can turn half incorporeal, shadowy and indistinct, and while it holds that state it shrugs off half of the harm from blades, clubs and arrows. It spreads ice across the ground, tunnels through rock and needs no air. It is unharmed by cold and throws off the first few spells that would bind it.
  • Weaknesses. No kind of harm hurts it more than another. Frost spells are wasted on it, and its half-incorporeal state takes concentration to hold.
  • Avoid or survive. Keep clear of desolate moons with fresh tunnels. Fire and force serve better than steel while it is shadowed.

Astral dreadnought

Lethal
  • Signs. A titanic creature covered from head to tail in thick, spiked plates, with two gnarled limbs ending in pincer claws and a single great eye in which constellations seem to swirl. Few who sail the drift ever see one.
  • Behaviour. Astral dreadnoughts have glided through the drift since the dawn of the multiverse, trying to devour every creature they meet. Whatever one swallows goes into a space of its own: a stone cave about a thousand feet across, with air and gravity, holding the remains of everything it has eaten before.
  • Strengths. Its eye casts a cone of dead magic about 150 feet long, as a beholder's central eye does, in whichever direction it chooses. Its claws and bite strike with force, and the bite swallows a creature that falls or is helpless. It can pull a creature it sees into its cave for a moment and spit it back, and it can loose a wave of psychic force at everyone around it. It shrugs off half of the harm from mundane blades, clubs and arrows, and it cannot be charmed, frightened, paralysed, petrified, poisoned, stunned, knocked prone or worn down. It throws off the first few spells that would bind it, and it needs no air, food, drink or sleep.
  • Weaknesses. No kind of harm hurts it more than another. Enchanted weapons strike it fully, but inside its cone of dead magic they strike as plain ones. The cone fails while its eye is shut or it is blinded. A creature swallowed into its cave can leave only by magic that crosses between planes; when the dreadnought dies, the cave vanishes and everything inside is released.
  • Avoid or survive. If one is sighted, turn away and keep going. A crew does not fight an astral dreadnought.

Boarders and thralls

Raiders and reavers

Variable
  • Signs. An unidentified ship that changes course toward yours, runs without colours, or hides in an asteroid field, a derelict or the dark side of a moon. "Reaver" is Fleet slang for a crew that boards and gives no quarter.
  • Behaviour. Piracy in the drift is endemic and belongs to no single people or organisation. Most of it is opportunistic and small-scale, worked from asteroid fields, derelict installations and the dark outer reaches of a system against cargo ships and under-armed transits.
  • Strengths. Surprise, and the choice of when to close. A boarding begins the moment two envelopes merge.
  • Weaknesses. Most raiders want cargo, and a ship that looks costly to take is usually left alone.
  • Avoid or survive. Identify at distance and keep separation. Weigh numbers and guns before anything else, and keep out of boarding range until the situation is clear.

Umber hulk

Dangerous
  • Signs. A hulking, insect-like burrower with steel-hard chitin, great claws, snapping mandibles and bewildering eyes. Neogi keep them as thralls and bring them on boardings.
  • Behaviour. It tunnels through rock, leaving a passage behind it, and lies in wait behind a wall or bulkhead, feeling for movement through the surface, before it bursts out. Survivors often remember little of the attack.
  • Strengths. Its gaze scrambles the mind of anyone within about thirty feet who meets its eyes: a victim stands dazed, stumbles off in a random direction, or strikes whoever is nearest, friend or foe. It claws and bites hard, senses movement through the ground or the deck, and sees in the dark.
  • Weaknesses. A sailor who averts their eyes is safe from the gaze, though they cannot see the hulk until they look again, and looking brings the gaze back. It has no special defence against any kind of harm, and where a neogi holds it by the neogi's own power, that hold breaks if the neogi dies or moves more than a mile away.
  • Avoid or survive. Fight it with your eyes lowered and trust your crewmates' calls. Kill or drive off its neogi master.

Vampirates

Dangerous
  • Signs. Gaunt figures with milky-white eyes and skin as dry as parchment, some with a peg leg or a hook. They sail ships of their own and sleep in coffins, in crates of grave dirt or among the cargo in the hold. Their crews drink rum and sing grim chanteys.
  • Behaviour. Vampirates are the undead husks of pirates who refused to go quietly. They plunder ships and feed on the life energy of the crews, by touch or from a short distance. They look more robust the more they feed. A vampirate ship usually carries shadows, the remains of their victims.
  • Strengths. A vampirate drains life with a touch or from about thirty feet, and a humanoid it drains to death rises at once as a shadow. Vampirates also carry crossbows and climb walls and ceilings like spiders. They are unharmed by cold, necrotic harm and poison, and cannot be charmed, poisoned or worn down. They need no air.
  • Weaknesses. Radiant light cuts twice as deep. A destroyed vampirate bursts into ash that can wither anyone standing next to it.
  • Avoid or survive. Fight them with radiant light where you have it, and step back from one as it falls.

Derelicts

Derelict ships

Hazard
  • Signs. A ship adrift that ignores hails, runs dark and shows an empty deck.
  • Behaviour. A derelict holds whatever killed its crew or drove them off, whatever has moved in since, and anyone else who got there first. Salvage crews, scavvers, shadows and raiders all work derelicts.
  • Strengths. Darkness, cover and narrow spaces, all of which favour what lives there.
  • Weaknesses. A derelict's envelope is often fouled or deadly, and its own occupants need air like anyone else.
  • Avoid or survive. Circle it before you close, and judge the state of its air before you merge envelopes, because merging shares the air out between both ships. Board in pairs with light, keep a line back to your own ship, and post a watch on your own deck.

Shadow

Dangerous
  • Signs. A patch of darkness that moves on its own and does not match the light. Shadows haunt derelicts and wrecks, and are often the remains of their crews.
  • Behaviour. It hides in dim light and darkness, slips through gaps an inch wide, and strikes from the dark.
  • Strengths. Its touch does necrotic harm and drains strength. The drained strength returns with rest, but a victim drained of all of it dies, and a humanoid killed this way rises as a new shadow a few hours later. Acid, cold, fire, lightning and thunder do it half harm, and so do mundane blades, clubs and arrows. Necrotic harm and poison do it none. It cannot be frightened, grappled, held, paralysed, petrified, poisoned, knocked prone or worn down.
  • Weaknesses. Radiant light cuts twice as deep. In sunlight a shadow strikes, acts and resists poorly.
  • Avoid or survive. Carry bright light and radiant spells into derelicts, and use enchanted weapons where you have them. A sailor who has been drained should rest before going back in.

Beholder-kin

Beholders themselves are covered under Beholders and kin in Peoples of the Drift. Like a beholder, each of these kin hovers and cannot be knocked prone.

Spectator

Dangerous
  • Signs. An orb about four feet across with one wide central eye and four eyestalks, two on each side. It speaks mostly mind to mind, out to about 120 feet.
  • Behaviour. A spectator is summoned by ritual to guard a place or a treasure, and it guards it for a hundred and one years, admitting nobody but its summoner unless told otherwise. If the treasure is stolen or destroyed before its term is up, the spectator vanishes. A spectator whose term has ended is free, and many stay on where they served and guard whatever they choose. It is civil on guard and will talk about its orders, though years alone give it odd habits: imaginary enemies, speaking of itself by name, borrowing its summoner's voice. Two spectators almost always fight to the death.
  • Strengths. Each turn it fires up to two different rays at targets out to about ninety feet: confusion, which makes a victim stand and strike at random; paralysis; fear; and a wounding ray of necrotic harm. When it resists a spell, or a spell attack misses it, it can turn that spell on another creature nearby, the caster included.
  • Weaknesses. Its bite is feeble; its danger is in its eyes. It has no special defence against any kind of harm.
  • Avoid or survive. Talk first; a spectator will often explain what it guards. Rely on weapons rather than spells against it.

Gazer

Hazard
  • Signs. A beholder in miniature, about eight inches across, with four eyestalks. It mimics speech it has heard in a high, mocking voice.
  • Behaviour. Gazers follow beholders like devoted, aggressive pets, and packs of them patrol a beholder's lair. A wild gazer eats bugs and small animals, avoids fights with anything person-sized unless it has a pack, follows and mocks travellers in its territory, and flees anything it cannot kill. It rushes toward a foe it sees.
  • Strengths. Each turn it fires two random rays at targets out to about sixty feet: a dazing ray that slows and fumbles its victim, a fear ray, a frost ray of cold harm, and a telekinetic ray that shoves a creature away.
  • Weaknesses. Fragile; one solid hit usually finishes it. It has no special defence against any kind of harm.
  • Avoid or survive. Kill it quickly, and expect a beholder nearby.

Hazards of the crossing

Air envelopes

Lethal
  • Signs. Air in an envelope passes through three stages. Fresh air is clean. Fouled air is stale and close, and everyone breathing it works clumsily and thinks slowly. Deadly air exhausts everyone who breathes it, worse with every minute, until they die.
  • Behaviour. A ship carries as much air as its size allows. Every breather aboard uses it up, so the more aboard, the sooner it fouls; fouled air lasts as long as the fresh air did before it turns deadly. When two envelopes touch, the air shares out evenly between them, and that is also how boardings begin.
  • Strengths. Fouling creeps up on a crew.
  • Weaknesses. A ship's air is fully restored when its envelope overlaps a world's atmosphere. Air pockets around asteroids restore a little.
  • Avoid or survive. Count breathers before a crossing and plan the route around the air. Refresh at every world you pass. Merge with another envelope only when you know its state and its ship's intent; merging with a fouled envelope fouls your own. Once the air turns foul, make for the nearest world.

Overboard

Hazard
  • Signs. Every ship has its own gravity: a plane running through its hull along the keel.
  • Behaviour. Someone who falls overboard drops toward that plane, overshoots it, is pulled back, and bobs back and forth across it until they come to float at it, alongside the ship and out of reach of the deck.
  • Avoid or survive. Rig lines and secure gear before work at the rail. Keep a line and a hook ready to recover anyone who goes over, and recover them quickly.

Color pools

Hazard
  • Signs. Portals adrift in the Astral Sea, each opening onto another plane.
  • Behaviour. Pools open and close quickly, and the Fleet charts them where it can.
  • Avoid or survive. Treat a pool on the course as an obstacle and clear it by a wide margin.

Dead gods and drifting matter

Hazard
  • Signs. The corpses of dead gods drift through the Astral Sea, with wreckage, debris and abandoned works collected around them. On an otherwise featureless crossing they serve as landmarks.
  • Behaviour. They are rarely empty. Salvage crews, hermits and githyanki all work them, and the wreckage offers cover to anyone waiting for a ship.
  • Avoid or survive. Expect company. Approach slowly, watch the debris for movement, and keep weapons manned until you know who is there.

Asteroid and debris fields

Hazard
  • Signs. Clusters of tumbling rock and drifting wreckage, often with small pockets of air among them.
  • Behaviour. Rocks shift and collide, and the field hides whatever is inside it: pirates, derelicts and, at the outer edge, the occasional astereater.
  • Avoid or survive. Plot a slow, careful course, post lookouts fore, aft and abeam, and keep the helm ready to turn.

Dead-magic and lethal worlds

Lethal
  • Signs. Most bodies in space have nobody living on them, and many could never support anyone. Some are lethal: crushing air, elemental storms, dead magic.
  • Behaviour. In dead magic, spells fail, though a caster's prepared power stays with them, unspent, until they leave. Gunpowder, blades and mundane gear keep working.
  • Avoid or survive. Survey a world from orbit before landing. Carry mundane weapons and tools as well as magic, and plan a way out that needs no spell.

Heat, cold and radiation near stars

Hazard
  • Signs. Beyond an envelope, temperature runs to extremes in both directions depending on how near the ship is to a star, and radiation varies with position.
  • Avoid or survive. Keep work beyond the envelope short, and keep well clear of stars.
